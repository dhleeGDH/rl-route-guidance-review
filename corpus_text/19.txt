ABSTRACT
Traffic congestion has become a prevalent phenomenon on urban roads, leading to significant challenges, including economic
losses due to travel delays, increased fuel consumption, and air pollution from vehicle emissions. As it is impractical to extensively
improve city road networks, vehicle routing optimization has emerged as a viable solution for alleviating congestion. However,
traditional algorithms cannot effectively process information for complex and changeable traffic environments. By contrast, deep
reinforcement learning (DRL) is a powerful approach for solving navigation problems. Rather than creating smart vehicles, we
propose a navigation model to guide all vehicles. The model employs a graph neural network to effectively capture dynamic traffic
flow patterns. We utilize the simulation of urban mobility to generate a large quantity of traffic data for use as reinforcement
learning samples. We propose a parallel simulation training strategy to accelerate DRL convergence. We verify the effectiveness of
our model by performing simulations on a simplified road network and a real-life road network under multiple traffic scenarios
and compare the results to those obtained using traditional methods and dynamic user equilibrium (DUE). The experimental
results demonstrate that the proposed model reduces average travel time by up to 7.54% and the number of halting vehicles by up
to 14.35% compared to traditional methods in high-congestion scenarios, maintaining stability across various traffic conditions.
The overall performance of the proposed method is comparable to that of DUE, indicating that traffic flow patterns mined through
the deep network can be used to effectively deduce the optimal vehicle route without performing the iterations required for DUE.
In general, the proposed approach has the capability to accommodate dynamic and complex traffic information, considerably
mitigating traffic congestion.

Introduction

In recent years, the continuous growth of car ownership has gradually produced an imbalance between traffic supply and demand,
resulting in increasingly severe traffic congestion. This congestion has produced an array of problems that cannot be ignored,

including economic losses incurred by individuals and increased
environmental pollution from vehicular emissions. Traffic congestion has been classified into recurring congestion (RC) and
nonrecurring congestion (NRC) [1]. Recurring congestion is typically attributed to a sudden surge in traffic volume and a shortage
of road capacity, such as during morning and evening peak hours,

At present, urban road networks are quite dense, making it challenging to implement large-scale network improvements. There
are two viable solutions for both RC and NRC: (1) predicting
future traffic volumes or the likelihood of accidents and implementing advance traffic control measures [2] and (2) implementing optimal path planning for vehicles, which would fully utilize
road network resources and mitigate the impact of traffic flow
on road sections [3]. Vehicle navigation software has a high market penetration rate; thus, utilizing this software to determine
the states of road networks would enable information acquisition
of the minutest arteries of urban road networks, providing data
support for route optimization. From a policy perspective, optimizing vehicle routing can reduce urban congestion, lower emissions, and enhance economic efficiency. This approach supports
smart city initiatives by leveraging existing navigation software to
implement centralized traffic management, potentially reducing
the need for costly infrastructure expansions. Therefore, optimal
path planning is a highly feasible solution to traffic congestion
and has been the subject of a growing number of studies in recent
years [4, 5].
The objective of vehicle navigation or path planning problems
is to minimize some type of loss, such as the travel time or
vehicle emissions. It is feasible to solve this problem by using
iterative optimization algorithms, such as genetic algorithms [6]
and ant-colony algorithms [7]. These algorithms can determine
a route assignment strategy with a low loss but cannot quickly
adapt to a dynamic traffic environment. An alternative solution
method involves the use of shortest path algorithms, such as Dijkstra [8] and A* [9], which are commonly employed in navigation
software. These algorithms have a high calculation speed and can
efficiently search for the optimal path within the current state of a
road network. However, these algorithms have a limited ability to
comprehend traffic flow patterns and may not always achieve an
optimal outcome in terms of the final loss. For navigation problems, it is necessary to develop an algorithm that can dynamically adapt to traffic changes and provide optimal paths for vehicles in real time. However, both iterative optimization algorithms
and traditional shortest path algorithms have clear limitations.
Some state-of-the-art algorithms [10, 11] only partially fulfill the
requirements for navigation while facing challenges in terms of
computational efficiency and optimality.
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a powerful
approach for addressing the limitations of traditional algorithms
in solving navigation problems. Unlike traditional algorithms,
DRL leverages deep neural networks to process complex traffic data and learn optimal routing strategies through continuous
exploration, making it suitable for dynamic urban environments.
From the perspective of reinforcement learning, navigation problems can be seen as the selection of different paths by vehicles
to achieve the overall optimum path, and the loss of each vehicle
(e.g., the travel time) can be viewed as the reward. Deep networks
can accommodate a vast quantity of inputs, capturing extensive traffic information and leveraging graphics processing units
(GPUs) to enhance the computational speed to meet real-time
requirements. Reinforcement learning (RL), as an evolutionary
2 of 17

learning method, enables continuous exploration of patterns in
samples and traffic flow evolutionary mechanisms. However,
existing reinforcement learning methods [12, 13] for vehicle navigation still face certain challenges, such as the case of heavy
traffic demand that traditional methods cannot meet. Moreover,
the training strategies proposed for deep networks have difficulty
tackling stochastic traffic demand.
To address the aforementioned challenges, we employ a navigation model based on Deep Q-Networks (DQN) [14] to manage
vehicle navigation, enabling it to adapt effectively to large-scale
road networks. Two kinds of inputs are considered in the model to
capture the dynamics of traffic flow. One input is the current state
of a road network, such as the average travel time for each road
section and the number of vehicles. The other input is the assignment of vehicles that have entered the road network; that is, the
number of vehicles assigned to each road segment is recorded
and accordingly deducted when a vehicle exits the road network.
As a traffic network has a graph-like structure, a graph neural
network is employed as the core architecture for capturing the
state of the road network using the aforementioned inputs. A
large number of samples are required to train a deep reinforcement learning network, and sufficient data are often not obtained
through real-world data collection. To address this issue, the simulation of urban mobility (SUMO) [15, 16] is used in this study
to generate a large volume of traffic data as training samples for
the proposed model. It is a common practice to use simulation
data to train reinforcement learning models and then transfer the
trained models to an actual environment, such as in the case of
autonomous vehicles [17] and intelligent robots [18]. The contributions of this study are summarized below.
• A deep learning network structure is designed to efficiently
extract comprehensive traffic information from large-scale
road networks for use in reinforcement learning.
• To accommodate the dynamic characteristics of traffic flow, a
parallel simulation strategy is proposed in which simulation
software is used to generate a large quantity of data, effectively harnessing the capacity of the deep network.
• Extensive experiments are performed to validate the feasibility and reliability of deep reinforcement learning in addressing navigation problems; demonstrating the generalization
ability of the proposed method.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2,
relevant research studies are presented and the study objective
is defined. In Section 3, we define the navigation problem from
the perspective of reinforcement learning and introduce a suitable model and training strategy. In Section 4, experiments are
described for simulating a simplified road network and a real-life
road network to test the proposed method under different traffic
flow conditions; the results are compared against those obtained
using other methods to validate the proposed method. Finally, the
key findings of this study are summarized and future research
directions are proposed.

Cai et al. [25] designed a system for analyzing trajectory data and
providing route inquiry recommendations for users. Similarly,

traffic-flow prediction and path planning are treated as separate tasks in these studies, disregarding the inherent interaction
between these tasks. For example, these methods predict future
traffic states based on historical traffic data, which may not align
with the path planning process. That is, the traffic patterns predicted by performing these two tasks separately may not always
be suitable for path planning. This mismatch can potentially lead
to navigation failures during heavy traffic flow.
Reinforcement learning is a methodology for determining a
response based on the environment and maximizing the expected
returns. This methodology has many applications in the fields
of intelligent control [27] and robotics [18]. Navigation problems
involve formulating the decision-making process of a vehicle for
choosing a path within the interactive context of the traffic environment and the vehicle itself. Consequently, RL is a suitable
research approach. In the field of RL, Q-learning [28] and Sarsa
[29] are widely recognized algorithms with many applications.
Gong et al. [30] introduced a method for local path planning

for ships based on Q-learning. Wang et al. [28] investigated the
application of problem-feature-based meta-heuristic algorithms
combined with Q-learning in urban traffic light scheduling, aiming to minimize the total network delay time by optimizing traffic signal scheduling. These algorithms constitute advances in
the field but encounter challenges in practical real-world navigation. For instance, similar to other traditional navigation algorithms, traditional RL methods have limitations in complex traffic environments.
In recent years, the rapid development of deep learning technology has led to important breakthroughs in various fields, such
as object detection [31] and natural language processing [32].
The integration of deep learning with RL has also resulted in
remarkable achievements, such as DQN surpassing human performance in gaming [14], AlphaGo Zero defeating human beings
[33], and protein structure prediction [34]. In the field of transportation, deep reinforcement learning has been applied to tackle
challenges in autonomous driving and vehicle lane changing.
For instance, Wu et al. [35] designed a mechanism for transferring control between drivers and autonomous driving agents
to correct unreasonable actions of an agent during the training process. Wang et al. [36] proposed a model to enhance the
safety and efficiency of lane-changing maneuvers in a mixed-flow
environment. Tigga et al. [37] proposed an adaptive traffic light
control system based on DQN, aimed at optimizing urban traffic signals using real-time traffic data to reduce vehicle waiting
time and queue length. However, effective vehicle navigation differs from autonomous driving in harnessing group intelligence.
That is, vehicle paths are selected to achieve maximum overall
returns based on global information. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have incorporated DRL into vehicle navigation to prevent traffic congestion. Koh et al. [13] developed a
method by which a vehicle selects actions (turning left or right
or moving straight) at every intersection based on the destination to minimize the travel time. Shou et al. [38] proposed a
new modeling paradigm for dynamic traffic assignment based
on Markov games and multi-agent reinforcement learning, used
to simulate the learning behavior of agents in route selection
and the system equilibrium process. Although the effectiveness
of using DRL for navigation problems was demonstrated, the
potential of the method to handle complex and dynamic traffic environments or large-scale road networks was not fully
exploited.
To overcome the limitations of traditional navigation methods
and address the shortcomings of DRL in navigation problems,
a deep network architecture is introduced in this study that can
capture complex traffic flow patterns and generate optimal routes
for vehicles. Extensive experiments are conducted to demonstrate
the advantages of the DRL model, which effectively overcomes
the limitations of previous models.

are usually used to find the optimal traffic assignment rather than
for real-time navigation. Some improved algorithms have been
developed. Cong et al. [23] proposed a novel ant-colony algorithm

links and routes are eliminated to enable ants to search the road
network more efficiently, striking a balance between the calculation speed and performance. Pasandi et al. [24] introduced a
method considering numerous factors with different weights to
calculate the optimal path for a pedestrian tourist. The method
can also be utilized to determine suitable paths for vehicles. However, the calculation speed and the number of required parameters make it difficult for this algorithm to process dynamic traffic
information. Consequently, the calculation speed and accuracy of
the algorithm may be considerably compromised for a medium to
large-scale road network with a large traffic flow.

Traditional navigation algorithms, such as Dijkstra, navigate a
vehicle with the lowest cost based on the current conditions.
However, the actual cost of traveling along the chosen path may
be higher than the initially estimated cost, leading to the selection
of a suboptimal path. Thus, this study develops a more effective
method to determine the optimal path based on road network
states.
Given the dynamic nature of traffic environments, it is challenging to employ a simple algorithm to determine the optimal
path under various scenarios. Therefore, it is crucial to accurately
characterize the states of road networks by capturing traffic flow
patterns to calculate the optimal path. To meet this objective,
the powerful ability of DRL to capture and model the complex
dynamics of road network states is leveraged for vehicle navigation in this study.
DRL consists of four primary elements: an agent, observation,
action, and reward. In this study, these four elements are defined
as follows.

origin–destination (OD) pairs, each with multiple feasible paths.
In this study, the agent calculates action values for all OD pairs
rather than individual vehicles. That is, the agent takes the current observation as the input and outputs action values for different paths associated with each OD pair. Therefore, a vehicle
entering the road network can select a path according to its OD
and the corresponding action values. This process is represented
by the following equation:

Conclusion

In this study, a deep reinforcement learning model is introduced for real-time vehicle navigation that effectively mitigates

congestion and minimizes travel time. Instead of focusing on
making vehicles smarter, we implement a navigation model to
manage all vehicles. To efficiently extract traffic features, we use
graph neural networks as the foundational model. Furthermore,
by incorporating traffic assignment data as input, we empower
the neural network to anticipate future traffic variations, ultimately enabling the model to devise superior navigation choices
for alleviating traffic congestion in a more effective manner.
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our model, we conducted
extensive experiments with diverse traffic demands. Experimental results show that the proposed method outperforms common
pathfinding algorithms and can be applied effectively to various traffic scenarios. Compared to DUE, the proposed method
achieves comparable results without performing iterations; it
is therefore more practical for real-world navigation. In certain
heavy-traffic scenarios investigated in the experiments, navigating vehicles to select detour paths in advance can alleviate overall
road network congestion, which is challenging to implement in
conventional algorithms.
However, the proposed method requires knowledge of vehicle
assignment, which may not be feasible to obtain in practice.
Therefore, future work could focus on removing this input
to enhance the practicality of the method. To better adapt to
real-world road networks, signal timing of intersections will
be incorporated in subsequent work to achieve collaborative
optimization. We also plan to integrate the model with smart
city policies, such as real-time traffic data sharing and public
transit coordination, to enhance its impact on urban mobility
and sustainability.
