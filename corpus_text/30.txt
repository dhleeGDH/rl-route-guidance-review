Abstract
Dynamic rerouting has been touted as a solution for urban traffic congestion.
However, its implementation is stymied by the complexity of urban traffic. To
address this, recent studies suggest the efficacy of novel technologies like fog
computing and deep reinforcement learning. However, there exist significant
challenges in this regard: (1) sorting massive amounts of data associated with
large urban networks, (2) large action space that hinders learning efficiency,
(3) impairment of rerouting efficacy due to overreliance on regional/local information, and (4) the issue of congestion shifting. To overcome these challenges,
this paper presents a novel two-step approach that integrates graph attention Q
network (GAQ) with entropy-balanced k shortest path (EBkSP) via fog-cloud
information framework to carry out vehicle rerouting in dynamic traffic environments typical of large cities. The first challenge is addressed by GAQ that
uses attention mechanism to evaluate traffic and assign region indices based
on the relative importance of information. The second challenge is addressed
using fog nodes that reduce the action space, and the third is addressed using
fog computing combined with cloud computing to facilitate the computation
of global optimal information in a centralized planning decentralized execution pattern. The fourth challenge is addressed using EBkSP, which identifies
vehicle optimal routes based on route popularity and vehicle priority. After
conducting a numerical experiment involving two other cutting-edge vehicle
rerouting models as baselines, it was established that the proposed model delivers
results that are 14%–54% superior from perspectives of the mean travel speed and
congestion. The superior efficacy of the proposed learning-based (compared to
rule-based) models is pronounced when rerouting ratios are low. As the rerouting
ratio increases, both the learning-based and rule-based model outcomes exhibit
reduced likelihoods of severe congestion. However, the learning-based model
consistently outperforms the rule-based model across all scenarios. The proposed

INTRODUCTION

1.1
Urban congestion mitigation
strategies
Urbanization continues to exacerbate traffic congestion,
negatively impacting urban commuting. Over 50% of the
world’s population resides in cities, set to rise to 80% by
2050 (United Nations, 2018). With over 1.5 billion vehicles worldwide, motorization continues to outpace urban
road expansion, and both demand-side and supply-side
solutions are being sought by transportation agencies.
Strategies to manage congestion can be placed into three
broad categories (US Department of Transportation, 2012):
providing more capacity (supply), reducing demand by
promoting favorable travel and land-use patterns, and
utilizing existing supply (capacity) more efficiently.
The first category involves building new corridors or
adding to existing lanes. However, this often leads to
induced demand, which ultimately defeats the purpose
of the intervention. The second category, travel demand
reduction, includes initiatives such as carpooling and congestion pricing (Jung et al., 2016). The latter is a promising
solution but could lead to inequitable or narrowly framed
outcomes (Eliasson & Mattsson, 2006; Goulden et al., 2014;
Owens, 1995). The third category leverages Information
and Communication Technology (ICT) and artificial intelligence (AI; through vehicle automation and connectivity),
the Internet of Things, and related advanced technologies,
all supported by real-time traffic monitoring, to optimize
the use of existing roadway capacity. These developments
are altering the transportation landscape in a profound
manner (Adeli & Jiang, 2009; Hashemi & Abdelghany,
2018; NHTSA, 2011).
The integration of ICT and AI in civil engineering
has significantly enhanced various disciplines, including
transportation, construction, and structural engineering
(Adeli, 2001; Adeli & Wu, 1998; Adeli & Yeh, 1989; X. Jiang
& Adeli, 2005; Rafiei & Adeli, 2016; Rafiei et al., 2017). In
transportation, ICT and AI enable real-time traffic information for road users, facilitate vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V)
and vehicle-to-infrastructure interactions, and leverage
technologies like Wi-Fi, smartphones, and cellular networks to improve connectivity and gather precise road
environment data (S. Chen et al., 2023; X. Jiang & Adeli,
2004; Mittal et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2022; D. Zhao & Liu, 2022;

Zheng et al., 2021). This increased vehicle connectivity provides essential real-time traffic data crucial for congestion
control efforts (Guériau et al., 2016; Ha et al., 2023; Mousavi
et al., 2021).
One of these advanced technology-based initiatives for
congestion mitigation is vehicle rerouting. In investigating
the efficacy of vehicle rerouting, the present study evaluates existing models and addresses challenges including
dynamic traffic and data overload. This study examines the
applicability of novel technologies like V2V connectivity
(Ha et al., 2020; Olovsson et al., 2022), deep reinforcement learning (RL), and attention mechanisms to vehicle
rerouting.

1.2

Optimize capacity–Vehicle rerouting

Vehicle rerouting (also known as vehicle routing guidance) is an intelligent transportation system (ITS)-related
initiative that has proven to be efficacious in reducing
congestion through dynamic traffic management and facilitates capacity utilization of existing roads (Liao, 2017).
Typically, at urban networks, specific corridors tend to
experience congestion, while others are often relatively
clear; this provides an opportunity to reroute vehicles to
avoid more congested areas.
Widely applied in everyday contexts, rerouting application examples include Google Maps and TomTom that suggest to their customers, optimal routes for time-efficient
travel (Wang et al., 2016). Dynamic real-time platforms,
such as Variable Message Signs in cities, redirect drivers
based on current traffic conditions (Zeng et al., 2020). A
demonstrated drawback of the above approaches is that it
leads to uniform rerouting guidance for drivers with comparable locations and intended destinations. As a result,
drivers adopting the same recommended route inadvertently amplify traffic flow on specific links, leading to the
translocation of congestion from one part of the network
to another (Tang et al., 2020).
To address congestion shifting, researchers have developed rerouting algorithms using techniques like anticipatory route guidance and multiple-route planning. Chen
and Underwood used dynamic programming to develop an
anticipatory system that minimizes time-dependent driver
costs and identified challenges including efficient updating of link costs and accounting for diversion timing (K.

DU et al.

Chen & Underwood, 1991). Bottom’s framework ensures
consistent information generation through fixed-point
problems (Bottom, 2000) and acknowledges problems of
scalability and adaptability associated with urban networks and dynamic traffic. Brennand et al.’s assignment of
K routes randomly to vehicles achieved systemwide optimality (Brennand et al., 2016) but may not be ideal for
individual drivers and could worsen congestion. Pan et al.’s
entropy-balanced k shortest path (EBkSP) algorithm allocates vehicles efficiently (Pan et al., 2013), but its success
hinges on driver compliance. In other work, researchers
have accounted for network uncertainties and developed
centralized and decentralized optimization algorithms for
vehicle rerouting guidance (Farver & Chabini, 2003; Gao &
Chabini, 2002). Some of these algorithms adeptly manage
slight network variations in small-scale settings. Nevertheless, their performance and computational efficiency
regarding larger networks with real-time information
remain uncertain.
The key challenges in traditional routing algorithms, as
recognized in most previous studies, include: (1) the need
for efficient communication, (2) the difficulty in attaining optimal solutions for extensive urban networks, (3)
the limited adaptability to rapidly changing environments,
and (4) non-consideration of driver compliance (Brennand
et al., 2016; Doolan & Muntean, 2013; Wang et al., 2016).

1.3
Improved Deep Reinforcement
Learning (DRL) with fog-cloud and
attention mechanism
To address the issue of traditional routing algorithms, DRL
is engaged in this study to allow direct acquisition of optimal routing choices from the environment; this boosts the
adaptability to changes and scalability potential (S. Chen
et al., 2020; Ding et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2022; Ha et al.,
2022; Liu et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2021), in a bid to bridge the
gap in the literature regarding the use of RL for dynamic
rerouting. In addition, in past literature, multiple agent RL
has been explored for vehicle path determination (Arokhlo
et al., 2011; Dong et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2020; J. Zhao et al.,
2022).
DRL holds promise for dynamic rerouting but faces challenges due to the vast action space, especially in complex
urban road systems. For instance, considering all possible
physical network links, the action space can reach a size
of 𝑎𝑒 , where “𝑎” is the total number of possible actions,
and “𝑒” is the total number of network links, making training time-consuming and potentially hindering algorithm
convergence. Additionally, the abundance of irrelevant
information in road traffic data slows down the learning
process. The complexity of urban road networks under-

To overcome these challenges, we adopt a fog-cloud

storage. This fog-cloud architecture accelerates information access, making it a promising choice for dynamic
rerouting (Du et al., 2022).
Recent studies, such as Brennand et al.’s ITS fog-based
architecture, have recognized the vast potential of fog
computing in dynamic rerouting (Brennand et al., 2017).
However, communication gaps between fog nodes might
restrict optimal routing to local (rather than global) optimality. Researchers have incorporated fog computing in
ITS congestion-mitigation strategies, but localized computation may hinder the achievement of system-wide
effectiveness (Cao et al., 2019). Combining fog and cloud
computing has shown promise; nevertheless, the computational limitations associated with fog computing (compared to cloud computing) remain a challenge (Rezaei
et al., 2018). Therefore, in this study, we introduce a
fog-cloud architecture with centralized control and decentralized execution, in a bid to overcome these limitations.
By doing this, we promote efficient and effective information sharing, which helps to ultimately realize dynamic
rerouting in an urban environment.

1.5

Framework description

Our dynamic rerouting framework, an extension of the
DRL + fog-cloud architecture introduced by Du et al.
(2021) leverages fog and cloud computing for efficient
data gathering and analysis. Fog nodes collect decentralized data transmitted to the cloud, where an attention
mechanism selects vital information and predicts congestion levels, proactively guiding the rerouting framework
toward optimal routes for congestion relief. This adaptable
framework is well-suited for various urban transportation
networks in terms of size and complexity, capable of creating customized rerouting models by inputting network
data and fog node configurations. In this two-phase framework (Figure 3), network flow information serves as the
input to the DRL phase, with the fog paradigm network
depicted as a graph, where nodes represent individual fog
nodes, and edges represent the links connecting them.
In the DRL phase, the Markov decision process guides
agent decisions using state, action, and reward, with the
central platform employing GAQ to create region indices
for road segments in fog node areas, serving as control
variables for vehicle rerouting. During route assignment,
region-level data are integrated into the decision-making
process, enabling the calculation of road weights based
on road vehicle density and region indices, which in
turn determine the 𝐾 shortest paths for each vehicle.
The allocation of optimal routes in the traffic network
utilizes an entropy-balanced approach, considering vehicle priority and route popularity to ensure efficient and
congestion-aware assignments. After routes are assigned,
the network’s state is updated, and data are collected for

The deployment of connectivity technology in vehicles, particularly in urban regions, continues to be limited
(Dong et al., 2021; Montanaro et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2022).
This makes it challenging to implement dynamic rerouting in most metropolitan areas. In this study, we define
two classes of vehicles with different capabilities regarding rerouting. The first class, termed rerouting vehicles
(RVs), can receive information through connectivity and
follow instructions accordingly. The second class, termed
background vehicles (BVs), does not have access to this
information and cannot be rerouted.
By considering both RVs and BVs in the numerical
experiments of this study, we seek to replicate real-world
scenarios and introduce dynamics into the traffic network.
RVs follow rerouting instructions, while BVs lend diversity
and unpredictability to the traffic network. Additionally,
both BVs and RVs have various origins and destinations,
and they can be identified by the fog nodes.

1.6.1

Agent

At every discrete time interval 𝑡, which we set at 200 time
steps, each fog node 𝑖 engages in a decision-making process
to select various actions {𝑎𝑖𝑡 , 𝑖 = 1, .., 𝑁}. These actions
are chosen based on a careful assessment of the prevailing
environmental conditions and are guided by a predefined
policy. The deliberate choice of a time interval of 200 time
steps is particularly salient as it provides the system with
adequate duration to respond to the outcomes of previous
decisions.
After the fog nodes have executed their selected actions,
the system transitions to a new state, and the participating
agents are subsequently rewarded based on this updated
state. These rewards serve as reinforcement, motivating
the agents to continuously refine their decision-making
strategies in pursuit of favorable outcomes.

1.6.3

Action space

Based on the collected state space (network feature and
node feature), at every time step, each fog node receives

five action choices known as region indices, representing
different potential congestion levels within their respective areas. These congestion levels are categorized as: 0
for free flow, 1 for light congestion, 2 for moderate congestion, 3 for heavy congestion, and 4 for standstill, which
can be represented as: 𝑎𝑖 = {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}. The cloud utilizes
RL to aggregate all the possible actions from each fog node
into the action space  = [𝑎𝑖 ], 𝑖 = 1, … , 𝑁. These actions
contribute to a concise, region-based congestion distribution insight, helping to comprehend the dynamics of traffic
congestion.
During the route-assignment phase, the road weight calculation is heavily reliant on the region index, which is
used to help generate route assignments for vehicles that
require rerouting.

1.6.4

Reward function

Congestion occurs when there are too many vehicles on
a roadway segment at a time, which results in speeds
significantly lower than normal (US Department of Transportation, 2012). Thus, congestion is often characterized by
a sudden reduction in the average speed of vehicles. The
reward calculation was derived from the collective average
speed of the RVs within specific regions, with a focus on the
regional level rather than the individual link level. To distinguish between substantial congestion and flowing traffic, we establish the threshold speed of 11 mph. This threshold was chosen with the intention of distinguishing RV
movement conditions effectively. RVs operating at sluggish
speeds (below 11 mph) are suggestive of severe congestion

1.7

Route-assignment phase

In the route assignment phase, RVs are recommended
to follow appropriate paths based on road weights and
vehicle positions. This process consists of two phases
(Figure 4): route computation and route selection. In the
route computation phase, K shortest paths are calculated
for each rerouted vehicle, considering its present location. In the route selection phase, a balanced-entropy
technique is applied to choose suitable routes from the
computed K alternative shortest paths, considering two
crucial aspects: the vehicle’s priority and the route’s popularity. This approach helps prevent the translocation (shift)
of congestion from one region to another.
The priority of RVs, denoted by 𝑅𝑉 =
(𝑅𝑉1 , 𝑅𝑉2 , … , 𝑅𝑉𝑛 ), is determined based on the distance between their current position and destination.
High-priority RVs may choose the shortest route irrespective of that route’s popularity, while low-priority RVs
must select the least popular route to avoid congestion
shift. Two different standards are used to calculate the

METHODOLOGY
DRL model architecture

At this phase, the architecture is designed in conjunction with the fog-cloud infrastructure, using a centralized
learning decentralized execution methodology. At each
time step, fog nodes receive different actions, aiming to
reduce congestion and to increase the efficiency of RVs.
GAT is applied to help select relatively important information and deep Q learning is used as the decision
processor.
The model input is the state (𝑠𝑡 ) at each time step 𝑡,
which is a tuple consisting of fog nodes feature matrix 𝑋𝑡
(with dimension 𝑁 × 𝐹, 𝐹 denotes the number of features
that are taken into account within a fog node area, where 𝑁
is the number of fog nodes) and adjacency matrix 𝐴𝑡 (with
dimension 𝑁 × 𝑁). Features considered within a fog node
area incorporate two elements: the average speed and the
degree of congestion. These data are collected through the
fog nodes.

3.1

Simulation settings

Precisely setting SUMO’s parameters is crucial for accurate results. Subsections cover network characteristics,

3.1.1

Network characteristics

The proposed framework was demonstrated using a dense
study network covering 5.926 square km in Manhattan.
This selection is driven by two key factors. First, Manhattan experiences severe congestion, making it a relevant
and challenging real-world scenario to test the effectiveness of our framework in mitigating congestion-related
issues. Second, the high road density in the Manhattan

area presents a complex and intricate transportation network that poses a significant challenge for the rerouting
problem.
The Manhattan network comprises 287 roads (edges)
and 120 intersections (nodes), encompassing both oneway and two-way roads with varying numbers of lanes. To
ensure realistic simulations, we obtained information from
OpenStreetMap, aligning the speed limits in the simulated
network with real-world conditions. The speed limits in
the network vary depending on the type of road, ranging
from 11 to 28 m/s.

3.1.2

Scenario settings

To replicate the dynamic characteristics of an urban road
network and introduce intricacy to the analysis, the designated study zone features several entryways for BVs and
RVs, each characterized by distinct travel patterns and endpoints. There are 25 BV entry locations with three moving
directions. (i) entry from the middle, moving to the top;
(ii) entry from the right, moving to the left; (iii) entry from
the middle, moving to the bottom; and (iv) entry from the
left, moving to the right. RVs enter the network via three
roads on the right of the network, with two destinations on
the left of the network (one from the bottom and the other
from the middle).
Throughout the phase of training, the rates of vehicle
inflow for both RVs and BVs are established at a rate of
100 veh/h. One of the significant factors that affect rerouting performance in mixed traffic is the RV penetration rate.

In the beginning, the model’s performance was tested in
five different situations, each with varying rerouting ratios.
These ratios ranged from 10% to 90%: 10% meaning 10% RVs
and 90% BVs, and so on, up to 90% RVs and 10% BVs. The
plans for BV trips remained consistent across these tests.
The flow of vehicles entering the network was a critical
consideration in these experiments as it directly influenced
the traffic dynamics and ultimately the road congestion
The study examined five different rates at which vehicles
entered the network (at all 28 specific entry points). This
rate varied from 50 to 150 veh/h at each entry point. Overall, this added up to a network-wide inflow ranging from
1400 to 4200 veh/h.
Next, the study explored different total numbers of vehicles (1000, 1500, 2000) in the tests. Three scenarios were
created, each representing a different number of vehicles, while the proportion of RVs in the mix remained the
same. Last, to further gauge the robustness of the proposed
framework, additional investigations (involving more OD
pairs and diverse OD matrices) were undertaken. The OD
pairs expanded from an initial count of 28 to 50 and 75
pairs, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of
the model’s performance.
The performance metrics used to evaluate the models
under the various scenarios are: (1) the mean speed and (2)
the probability of encountering severe traffic congestion.
As shown in Figure 8a, the proposed model (represented

by the solid white-colored bars in the figure) demonstrates
superior performance in terms of average speed across
various rerouting ratios. It exhibits a considerable improvement of 14% and 54% over the baseline models while also
showcasing enhanced proficiency in reducing the probability of severe congestion by 8% and 21% (see Figure 8b
and Table 5).
It is worthy to note that the learning-based models (that
is, the white-colored and hatched bars, viz., GAQ-EBkSP
and GCQ-EBkSP) consistently outperform the rule-based
model (dotted bars in the figure) across all the scenarios considered. The rule-based model hinges solely on
redirecting vehicles based on existing network density,
disregarding congestion potentially existing at other road
sections. Consequently, in the rule-based model, vehicles
lack proactive “future planning,” rendering them incapable of collaboratively selecting optimal routes. On the
other hand, the learning-based models inherently possess
more favorable reward profiles. Furthermore, the superior
efficacy of the learning-based models becomes particularly
pronounced when rerouting ratios are low, signifying that
fewer vehicles follow the recommended alternative routes;
this increases the probability of encountering significant
congestion.
In Figure 8b, it is evident that at lower rerouting ratios
(≤30%), the learning-based model exhibits a 43% probability of encountering severe traffic congestion, while
the rule-based model displays a higher probability of
56% (13% greater than the learning-based model) in such

diverse OD matrices. The fixed parameters of rerouting
ratio, inflow rate, and vehicle quantities remain consistent
(30%, 2800 veh/h, and 1000 vehicles). In earlier setups, the
arrangement of OD matrices was designed to create congestion within the network, with a focus on four primary

In the first three testing scenarios, we maintain these
travel directions but increase the number of OD pairs to

performance level (even when the OD pairs are elevated to

model.

travel directions), the OD pairs in the randomized test
were selected randomly from the network. In the randomized test, the relative performance of the learning-based
model improves noticeably, with average speeds of 14.2 and
13.7 m/s, and the probability of encountering severe congestion drops to 55% and 60%, compared to the erstwhile
75 OD pairs test. Meanwhile, the rule-based model’s performance remains the poorest as indicated in Table 5. Overall,
this result underscores the benefits of incorporating DRL
into rerouting frameworks. The proposed model consistently outperforms other baseline models, highlighting the
effectiveness of embedding GAT within DRL frameworks.

In recognition of the highly dynamic nature of urban
traffic and the limitations of the rerouting guidance methods in the literature, this study proposes a fog-cloud

architecture-based GAQ-EBkSP model to improve learning efficiency and effectively reduce the incidence of congestion translocation (shifting) that is typically associated
with urban traffic rerouting guidance. With a fog-cloud
architecture and a graph attention-based DRL (GAQ),
the learning efficiency is boosted. The proposed model
also incorporates EBkSP techniques that help address the
limitations of previous methods by effectively assigning
routes to each vehicle based on its priority and the relative
popularity of routes within the network.
The study tested several scenarios using the Manhattan
(NY) road network. The baseline models used for comparison were two state-of-the-art models: a rule-based model
(EBsSP) and a learning-based model (GCQ-EBkSP). Based
on the testing outcomes, it is apparent that the attention learning-based model (GAQ-EBkSP) proposed in this

study outperforms the baseline models concerning the
mean speed and the probability of encountering significant traffic congestion in diverse scenarios. The suggested
model attains a 2.4 m/s higher mean speed and an 8%
decreased probability of encountering substantial traffic
congestion over the baseline GCQ-EBkSP model. In addition, when the network is very busy (a relatively high
number of vehicles in the network), the proposed GAQEBkSP has a 17.5% less probability of encountering severe
congestion, compared to the other learning-based baseline
model (GCQ-EBkSP).
The superior efficacy of the learning-based (compared
to rule-based) models is pronounced when rerouting ratios
are low. As the rerouting ratio increases, both learningbased and rule-based models exhibit reduced likelihoods
of severe congestion. However, the learning-based model

DU et al.

consistently outperforms the rule-based model across all
scenarios.
The pivotal role of the fog nodes layer stands out in
the proposed framework. In the context of this study,
a total of six fog nodes were deployed strategically to
cover the network area. It is worth noting that the
telecommunication technology employed by the fog computing system can impact the number of fog nodes,
emphasizing the need for a closer examination. In light
of this, future research could delve into investigating
various quantities and sizes of fog nodes, taking into
account the influence of telecommunication technology.
This exploration would provide valuable insights into
how different fog node configurations affect the rerouting
instructions.
Furthermore, the efficacy of rerouting instructions
hinges on a thorough consideration of physical and institutional road attributes. These attributes include elements
such as facility type, land-use patterns, left-turn opportunities, and tolls. In future research, consideration of these
attributes in the route assignment process could contribute
to more reliable and efficient rerouting optimization for
vehicles in a congested urban network.
In future research, we plan to explore the effects of different time step intervals and assess their applicability in
capturing the dynamic nature of various urban scenarios
more comprehensively. Additionally, our proposed framework could be further fortified by incorporating other
rerouting considerations, such as proactiveness, predictive
capabilities, personalized considerations, driver response
to information, and consistent anticipatory route guidance that were introduced by pioneers in the field of
rerouting.
