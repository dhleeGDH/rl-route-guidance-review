Routing technologies have long been available in many automobiles and smart phones, but the nearly random nature
of traffic on road networks has always encouraged further efforts to improve the reliability of navigation systems.
Given the networks’ uncertainty, an adaptive dynamic route selection model based on reinforcement learning is
proposed. In the proposed method, the Markov decision process (MDP) is used to train simulated agents in a network
so that they are able to make independent decisions under random conditions and, accordingly, determine the set of
routes with the shortest travel time. The aim of the research was to integrate the MDP with a multi-nomial logit
model (a widely used stochastic discrete-choice model) to improve finding the stochastic shortest path by computing
the probability of selecting an arc from several interconnected arcs based on observations made at the arc location.
The proposed model, tested with real data from part of the road network in Isfahan, Iran, and the results obtained
demonstrated its good performance under 100 randomly applied stochastic scenarios.

Introduction

Streamlined traffic flow and transportation through arterial
roads can have major positive impacts on urban trade and the
economy and quality of city life. Depending on the objective
and destination, the behaviour of users of a road network can be
categorised into different classes, such as shopping, commuting,
business trips, cargo deliveries and so on. All these objectives
and destinations are usually reached by motor vehicles traversing
the ground, which thus require their own travelling routes.
Extreme demand for travelling routes leads to congestion on the
road network, longer travel times and increased noise and air
pollution, which require additional efforts and capital to counter
and control. Thus, methods of optimal routing and network balancing have emerged to minimise these adverse effects and
reduce travel times. Given the importance of optimal routing in
today’s traffic, this issue has been extensively researched, leading

Proceedings of the Institution of Civil Engineers - Transport

to the emergence of many routing methods and algorithms,
such as shortest path, flooding and so on. However, the need to
incorporate the randomness of road networks into routing has
necessitated the use of intelligent approaches.
In this paper, a new adaptive dynamic route selection (ADRS)
method is presented, which is based on intelligent agents (i.e.
an agent-based system) for the optimisation of travel routes.
Time, distance or passage through certain nodes can be considered as performance criteria and the basis for introducing
the algorithm to the agent, so that the route can be optimised
accordingly. Agent-based methods fall within a specific scientific domain called machine learning. An efficient method of
machine learning is reinforcement learning, and this method
was employed in this study to develop the ADRS model for
finding the optimal path in a network with variable route cost.
The main aim pursued in this study was to use reinforcement
learning innovatively in the ADRS approach. This raises the
controversial question of whether it is possible to make the
route selection approach intensively accurate. In addition,
route selection in agent-based systems is a stochastic process,
so is it feasible to amend the action selection process?
This research addressed these questions.
The findings of this study revealed that targeted route selection
in the process will modify the whole Markov decision process
(MDP). The results also showed that modified approach
exceeds the former and the whole process will take less time
than the MDP.

3.

ADRS methodology

Given that transport networks are totally stochastic, route
selection should be considered as a part of dynamic simulation
of traffic flows and networks simultaneously. The MDP is a
reinforcement-learning method that has been used in many
applications to estimate the state of a dynamic system. In
reinforcement-learning problems, an agent seeks to find the
best solution by experience and trial and error (Nguyen et al.,
2014; Zhang and Lesser, 2013). In other words, an agent
randomly selects an action from a set of available options and
performs it in a defined environment. In this work, this action
selection process is utilised for selecting the best sequence of
routes. The selection of a route by an agent changes the condition of the transportation network and the agent receives

a reward accordingly. This process is repeated until a desired

route selecting accurate and thus the results will become
more extensively accurate. The ADRS model was developed by
combining the MDP and a discrete-choice model, which leads
the solution to a cost-effective, more accurate and desirable
result. The whole process of the ADRS model is shown in

It is notable that the problem is viewed from the perspective
of a purposeful agent interacting with an uncertain
environment.

As shown in Figure 1, the ADRS process starts with the
input of the required parameters (initialisation). Network
initialising begins with the input of effective parameters of
arcs. Afterwards, agents evaluate the features and calculate
the probabilities of each arc. In the MDP, an agent selects
its routes (actions) randomly. However, the ADRS chooses
the routes according to the calculated probability of arcs in
the previous stage. Although routing progresses by trial and
error and the agent navigates the path that yields the
greatest reward, the rewards come from the action selection of
the previous stage and at every stage the goal is to receive the
greatest cumulative reward after all stages.

Two types of reward are considered
(a) rewards that approach an optimal value as iterations
progress and thus play a significant role in determination
of the optimal solution
(b) rewards that remain unchanged and are added to the
main reward function as a constant.
For a sequence of rewards, the characteristic of the ADRS
will be rt+k; this is the reward at time or stage t. It is notable
that rewards have an inverse relation with the cost function.
In other words, the higher the cost, the less the reward. An
agent seeks to maximise the expected value of rewards gained
from all actions.

4.

Case study

To evaluate the performance of the ADRS method in a
realistic problem, Visual Basic was used to code the method as
a plugin for ArcMap software. A map of district 3 of Isfahan
city, Iran, was used for testing. A diagram of the main routes
in this district was developed and information about the travel
time functions in these routes was obtained from the Isfahan
traffic centre. Other information about the links (shown in
Table 5) was obtained from a geo-database held by the Isfahan
Municipality (see Isfahan Municipality, 2015).
Unlike the numeral example, the incident ratios of links were
used in this case study. These were derived from the Isfahan
Municipality database. The incident ratio is the probability of
an incident occurring in a link and can thus be used as the
transition probability P(s′|s, a). Consideration of the transition
probability leads to a more stochastic situation, but one that is
closer to reality.
In this case study, 100 randomly generated scenarios were
performed in the mentioned network for three rounds. In each
round, 1000 agents were simulated, each of which used the
proposed ADRS method, the Nadi–Edrisi MDP (Nadi and
Edrisi, 2017) and the DPM for their route selection process.
The scenarios created stochastic situations such as different
start–end nodes and various incidents to test the performance of
the proposed model in comparison with the other two methods.
For example, in one of the scenarios, the objective was to find
the best route from Jomhuri Square to Enghelab Square.
Figure 4 shows the route identified as the best in all three
methods. The route is 3, 14, 19, 27, 39, 44, 48, 53. Table 6 lists
the maximum Q values without considering incidents on
Isfahan’s Chahar-Bagh-Abbasi Street using the ADRS
approach. The values are shown on each link in Figure 4.
In the next step, stochastic traffic incidents were applied on
arcs to evaluate the performance of the proposed model under
dynamic random situations. ‘Incidents’ include congestion
charge zones, low-emission zones and stochastic events such as
crashes and construction operations. These constraints do not
allow users to pass the limited paths for a specific time.

5.

Conclusion

With growing traffic congestion problems in many of the
world’s road networks, there is an increasing need for optimal
route selection methods to minimise delays in business travel,
shopping trips and so on. The use of route optimisation
methods can significantly contribute to the alleviation of road
congestion and thus smooth traffic flows. In this work,
an adaptive and dynamic method of optimal route selection
was developed in order to redistribute traffic flow under the
condition of incidents in the transportation network. Part of
the road network in Isfahan, Iran, was used as a test bed
and the results showed that the model had a total error of 9%
over 100 randomly generated scenarios. The proposed method
was found to outperform two other route selection methods.
