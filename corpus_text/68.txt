ABSTRACT With the growing urgency of carbon neutrality, sustainable intelligent transportation systems
must support green path planning that simultaneously accounts for arbitrary origin–destination (OD) pairs,
strict emission constraints, and large-scale concurrent requests. To address this challenge, this paper proposes
a Constrained Reinforcement Learning for Shortest Path (CRL-SP) framework, which generates real-time,
scalable, and emission-compliant routing solutions under hard carbon constraints. The framework adopts
a dual-tower Universal Value Function Approximation architecture based on Deep Q-Networks (UVFADQN), integrating topological embeddings with contextual features, and is trained via a progressive strategy
that combines demonstration-based behavior cloning with interactive reinforcement learning. Numerical
experiments on benchmark networks demonstrate that CRL-SP consistently recovers optimal or near-optimal
solutions, achieving path match rates and link overlap ratios typically above 0.85, while relative deviations in
travel time and emissions remain close to zero. The proposed model preserves this level of performance under
unseen and sparsely sampled emission budgets, and is able to generate feasible and structurally consistent
paths even when classical constrained shortest-path algorithms fail due to insufficient budgets. In terms of
computational efficiency, CRL-SP supports large-scale batch inference with throughput exceeding tens of
thousands of paths per second, yielding orders-of-magnitude speedups over traditional label-setting methods.
Overall, CRL-SP provides a practical and scalable learning-based solution for emission-constrained path
planning in sustainable intelligent transportation systems.

path-planning paradigms that focus solely on minimizing
travel time or distance are no longer adequate. Contemporary climate policies increasingly translate long-term
sustainability goals into concrete and enforceable operational
requirements. These are implemented through regulatory
mechanisms such as fleet-level carbon budgets, emissionbased pricing or charging schemes, and access control

Path planning algorithms form the cornerstone of optimizing travel efficiency within Intelligent Transportation
Systems (ITS). Traditional algorithms, such as Dijkstra’s
algorithm (1959) and the A* algorithm [1], have been
widely applied for finding shortest paths in network graphs.
Dijkstra’s algorithm guarantees optimality by exhaustively
exploring all nodes from a start point, while A* improves
efficiency through heuristic-guided search towards a goal.
Subsequent improvements have continued to refine these
foundational methods for specific applications, such as
system-level calibration path planning [2]. However, a common characteristic of these traditional approaches is their
primary focus on metrics like shortest distance or travel
time [3], which fails to address growing environmental
imperatives.
In response to the urgent need for sustainable transportation, research has evolved towards the ‘‘green vehicle
path problem’’. This shift has led to the development of
models that incorporate carbon emissions as a key objective.
For instance, [4] proposed a hybrid robust-stochastic model
considering cost, time, and emissions under uncertainty,
while [5] integrated distance, time, and emissions into a
multi-objective framework. Reference [6] further extended
this line of inquiry to multimodal transportation planning.
Nevertheless, a significant disconnect persists between these
multi-objective optimization paradigms and the practical
requirements of carbon neutrality policies. Real-world
regulations often impose absolute emission caps as hard
constraints—not merely soft objectives for optimization.
Most existing models treat emissions as one factor to be
traded off against others, rather than as a mandatory limit
that must not be violated, thereby limiting their applicability
in policy-driven contexts. Recent domain-specific methods

in policy-mandated scenarios.

vehicle fleet is a critical oversimplification. The carbon
footprint of a path is not intrinsic to the road link but
is a function of the vehicle traversing it. While a private
car might have low emissions on a segment, a heavy-duty
truck on the same path could exceed its carbon budget.

II. METHODOLOGY

In this study, the path planning task is formulated as
a sequential decision problem under a prescribed carbon
emission budget. Specifically, in a transportation network, for
arbitrary origin–destination (OD) pairs and a given emission
budget, the decision maker incrementally selects adjacent
links to construct a feasible path from the origin to the
destination, such that the total travel time is minimized
while the cumulative emissions do not exceed the budget
limit. In practical applications, this problem must be solved
repeatedly under continuously varying OD pairs and emission
budgets. This setting imposes stringent requirements on
the decision model, which must not only guarantee strict
constraint feasibility but also exhibit strong generalization
capability and computational efficiency.
To efficiently address the above emission-constrained path
decision problem, this study proposes the Constrained Reinforcement Learning for Shortest Path (CRL-SP) framework.
The framework integrates demonstration solutions of the
constrained shortest path problem with reinforcement learning, enabling generalized decision making across arbitrary
OD pairs and continuous emission budgets within a unified
modeling structure. A dual-tower Q-network architecture is
adopted to fuse embedding representations of network topology and destination information with continuous contextual
features. During decision making, an explicit feasible-action
VOLUME 14, 2026

Y. Wang et al.: Shortest-Time Path Planning Under Carbon Emission Constraints via RL

filtering mechanism is applied to ensure that emission
constraints are always satisfied. In the training stage,
demonstration paths generated by a constrained shortest path
algorithm are first utilized for behavior cloning pretraining
to establish stable decision preferences. This is followed by a
reinforcement learning phase, where Double DQN combined
with multi-step returns and prioritized experience replay
is employed to further refine policy performance. During
inference, the resulting end-to-end framework efficiently
generates near-optimal shortest-time paths while strictly
respecting carbon emission constraints.

budgets.

B. MDP FORMULATION AND FEASIBLE ACTION
DEFINITION

To incorporate the emission-constrained shortest-time path
problem into a reinforcement learning framework, it is first
necessary to establish a clear correspondence between the
original optimization formulation and a sequential decisionmaking process. In the classical problem, the objective
is to minimize cumulative travel time subject to a strict
emission budget, where path feasibility is governed by a hard
constraint rather than treated as a trade-off or penalty term.
Within the reinforcement learning setting, path construction
is therefore modeled as a stepwise decision process. Travel
time naturally corresponds to accumulated step costs, path
continuity is captured by the state transition dynamics, and
the emission budget is explicitly embedded into the state
and action feasibility definitions. Under this formulation,
constraint satisfaction is enforced directly at the decision
level by restricting the admissible action space, ensuring
that policy learning is strictly confined to the feasible
domain. It is important to emphasize that the emission
constraint is not handled through Lagrangian relaxation or
expectation-based constraint violation, as in conventional
Constrained Markov Decision Process formulations that
enforce constraints in expectation via dual variables. Instead,
learning and decision-making are conducted entirely within a
pruned state-action space that respects the hard emission limit
at all times.
46718

Based on this correspondence, the emission-constrained
shortest-time path problem can be formalized as a Markov
Decision Process (MDP). At each decision step, the agent
observes the system state defined by its current node and
remaining emission budget, and determines the feasible
action set by jointly considering the residual budget and
the minimum emission lower bound required to reach the
destination. The agent selects an action exclusively from this
admissible set. After execution, the agent advances along the
chosen link, the remaining budget is updated accordingly,
and the environment returns an immediate reward together
with the next state. The episode terminates when the
destination is reached successfully or when no feasible
action remains due to budget exhaustion or infeasibility.
Through this construction, the original path-search problem
is reformulated as a sequential decision process operating
within an explicitly constrained feasible domain, providing
a consistent MDP foundation for subsequent value-based
learning.

3) TRAINING CONFIGURATION

The proposed framework adopts a two-stage training strategy
consisting of a behavior cloning (BC) pretraining phase
followed by an online reinforcement learning phase. In the
BC stage, the model is pretrained using demonstration paths
obtained by solving the constrained shortest-path problem
(CSPP). Each demonstration path is decomposed into a
sequence of state–action pairs, which guide the model to
learn feasible and time-efficient action preferences under
emission constraints. This stage is conducted for a fixed
number of training epochs using mini-batch optimization,
without performance-based early stopping, to provide a stable
policy initialization for subsequent reinforcement learning.
46723

Y. Wang et al.: Shortest-Time Path Planning Under Carbon Emission Constraints via RL

After BC pretraining, the model proceeds to the online
reinforcement learning stage based on the Double DQN
framework, augmented with n-step returns and prioritized
experience replay (PER). Training in this stage is organized
in episodes. At the beginning of each episode, an origin–
destination pair and its corresponding emission budget are
randomly sampled. The agent then incrementally constructs a
path on the fixed network until it either reaches the destination
successfully or terminates due to constraint violation. The
resulting state transition samples are stored in a replay buffer
and are sampled according to their assigned priorities for
parameter updates.
Exploration is governed by an ε-greedy strategy, in which
ε is gradually decayed from an initial value to a small
constant over the course of training, encouraging exploration
in early stages and promoting convergence in later stages.
The target network is updated using Polyak averaging to
improve the stability of value estimation. The reward function
consists of stepwise travel-time penalties, a terminal reward
for successfully reaching the destination within the emission
budget, and a fixed penalty for infeasible termination, thereby
balancing path efficiency and feasibility.
The online reinforcement learning stage terminates after
a predefined maximum number of environment interaction
steps, and no validation-based model selection or early stopping criteria are employed. The model parameters obtained
at the end of training are directly used for evaluation to
avoid additional sources of uncertainty. All hyperparameters

4) BASELINE METHODS

To systematically evaluate the performance of the proposed method for the carbon emission-constrained shortest
path problem, several representative baseline methods are
considered for comparison. These baselines correspond to
three typical solution paradigms, namely exact or near-exact
optimization, an idealized time-performance reference, and
a low-complexity heuristic, thereby characterizing different
performance boundaries of the problem.
1) CSPP with Label-Setting Algorithm
A classical label-setting algorithm for solving the
constrained shortest path problem (CSPP) is adopted
as the primary optimization baseline. This method
maintains multiple labels for each node and applies
dominance-based pruning during the search process,
enabling the computation of optimal or near-optimal
paths under emission constraints. It serves as
an optimality-oriented reference for performance
comparison.
2) Emission-Agnostic Shortest Path
This baseline computes the shortest-time path between
an origin–destination pair by optimizing travel time
only and completely ignoring emission constraints.
46724

A standard shortest path algorithm is used for this
purpose. Owing to its optimality in terms of travel time,
this method provides an idealized upper bound on time
performance, although the resulting paths are generally
infeasible with respect to emission limits.
3) Greedy Feasible Heuristic
A low-complexity feasible heuristic is also included as
a baseline. This method constructs a path incrementally
using a myopic greedy rule. At each node, among
all outgoing links that satisfy the remaining emission
budget and preserve reachability to the destination (i.e.,
the link emission cost plus a precomputed emission
lower bound does not exceed the remaining budget),
the link with the minimum travel time is selected.
This heuristic ensures feasibility at each step but does
not incorporate any global optimization or look-ahead
VOLUME 14, 2026

Y. Wang et al.: Shortest-Time Path Planning Under Carbon Emission Constraints via RL

mechanism, and therefore does not guarantee global
optimality.
4) Lagrangian Relaxation Reinforcement Learning
(LR-RL)
To compare with standard soft-constraint approaches
in the constrained RL literature, we introduce a
Lagrangian relaxation RL baseline (LR-RL). This
method removes the feasible action mask and uses the
full action space. At each step, the reward includes a
Lagrangian penalty term based on the emission cost
of the selected link (−λ · ca ). The multiplier λ is
selected via grid search on the validation set to achieve
a reasonable trade-off between travel time performance
and constraint satisfaction. An episode terminates only
when the destination is reached or when the number of
steps exceeds twice the length of the longest acyclic
path for the current OD pair. The terminal rewards
for success and failure are kept identical to those in
CRL-SP.

G. ABLATION OF STRUCTURAL DESIGN

To further investigate the impact of key structural designs on
the learning process and final performance, targeted ablation
experiments were conducted to isolate the contributions of
constraint enforcement mechanisms, architectural variants,
and training strategies. These experiments systematically
evaluate the effects of each component on path construction
stability, convergence efficiency, and solution quality through
comparative analysis. Specifically, the ablation on constraint
enforcement mechanisms examines three progressive variants: the proposed hard constraint mechanism based on action
masking (the main method), a soft constraint scheme that
allows the full action space and applies a terminal penalty
upon violation, and the most basic unconstrained pure DQN
model (using only time-based rewards, without masking or
penalties). This comparison aims to analyze the role of the
hard constraint mechanism in path construction stability and
learning efficiency. The ablation on architectural variants
focuses on the dual-tower UVFA structure and a simplified
single-tower DQN scheme, evaluating the contribution of
topological embeddings and contextual feature fusion to
representational capacity. The ablation on training strategies
compares a progressive reinforcement learning framework
that integrates behavior cloning pretraining with a pure
reinforcement learning strategy initialized from scratch,
analyzing the role of behavioral priors in accelerating
convergence and enhancing stability in strongly constrained
environments. In all ablation settings, the remaining network architecture, training hyperparameters, and evaluation
procedures remain identical. To characterize the dynamic
evolution, key performance metrics (such as mean link
overlap ratio) are recorded at regular intervals on a fixed
evaluation set, and the contributions of different components are analyzed through comparisons of their training
trajectories.
Figure 13 illustrates the evolution of the path structural
similarity metric, measured by Mean LOR. The upper
panel (A) presents the results of the ablation on constraint enforcement mechanisms, while the lower panel (B)
corresponds to the ablation on architectural variants and
46734

training strategies. In the upper panel (A), the comparison
of constraint enforcement mechanisms shows that the hard
constraint mechanism based on action masking (the main
method) maintains the highest and most stable Mean LOR
values throughout the training process. Its performance
improves rapidly in the early stage and gradually converges
to a high and stable range in the later stage. In contrast,
the soft constraint scheme (which allows the full action
space and applies a terminal penalty upon violation) exhibits
lower structural similarity in the early stage, followed by
slower improvement accompanied by noticeable fluctuations;
even in the later stage of training, its Mean LOR remains
significantly lower than that of the hard constraint setting.
The pure DQN model (using only time-based rewards,
without any masking or penalties) displays the lowest and
nearly flat curve: a slight initial rise followed by rapid
stabilization at a low level, reflecting severe degradation of
path structure under completely unconstrained conditions.
These comparisons indicate that, in strongly constrained
path planning problems, reliance on post-hoc penalty signals
substantially increases exploration noise, whereas the hard
constraint mechanism, by pre-filtering infeasible actions,
effectively limits the search space and significantly enhances
training stability and structural convergence efficiency.
The results in the lower panel (B) further reveal the
synergistic effects of architectural variants and training
strategies. The dual-tower UVFA framework incorporating
behavior cloning pretraining (the main method) exhibits
high Mean LOR values from the initial training stage and
quickly enters a stable improvement regime, demonstrating
enhanced sample efficiency and improved initial feasibility. The single-tower DQN variant (with removal of the
independent tower structure) shows a slightly slower ascent
trajectory and stabilizes at a suboptimal level, indicating that
the separation and fusion of topological embeddings and
contextual features make an indispensable contribution to
representational capacity. In contrast, the pure reinforcement
learning strategy initialized from scratch (without behavior
cloning) exhibits lower structural similarity in the early stage
and a slower improvement rate, but as training progresses,
its Mean LOR gradually approaches the stable range of the
main method. This pattern suggests that behavior cloning
pretraining primarily accelerates early solution discovery
and convergence by providing a feasible structural prior,
while architectural simplification introduces a noticeable
bottleneck in final performance.
Overall, the results in Figure 13 demonstrate that different
structural designs play complementary roles in the learning
process: constraint enforcement mechanisms primarily determine training stability and the effective feasible search space,
architectural variants directly influence representational
capacity and final solution quality, and training strategies
mainly regulate sample efficiency and convergence speed.
These findings further validate the rationality and necessity
of the proposed framework for strongly constrained path
planning tasks.

IV. CONCLUSION

This study reformulates emission-constrained shortest-path
planning as a constrained reinforcement learning problem,
demonstrating the feasibility of learning-based paradigms
under stringent operational demands. The proposed CRL-SP
framework integrates feasibility-aware decision rules with
data-driven value approximation, enabling rapid adaptation to
heterogeneous origins and dynamic budgets while ensuring
strict compliance with hard constraints. This innovation
bridges the precision of traditional optimization algorithms
with the flexibility of AI, introducing a mechanism for intelligent response to environmental changes in transportation
systems.
Experimental insights reveal that CRL-SP aligns closely
with CSPP-optimal solutions across diverse budgets, even
capturing transferable routing patterns in unseen scenarios
rather than relying on memorized interpolation. This suggests
that reinforcement learning can implicitly encode global
trade-offs, surpassing explicit enumeration to provide more
robust solutions. Notably, under extreme constraints, the
framework’s conservative behavior, prioritizing feasibility
over unchecked optimality, underscores its potential as
a safety net in real-world transportation, where policy

VOLUME 14, 2026

stringency or resource scarcity may render traditional methods ineffective.
Practical advantages center on computational efficiency:
through lightweight forward propagation, CRL-SP achieves
order-of-magnitude throughput gains in batch queries, suitable for real-time dynamic routing and traffic management,
while potentially reducing computational energy use in
support of low-carbon objectives. However, limitations
include reliance on static network attributes, which fail to
fully capture the stochastic and time-varying nature of actual
traffic systems; behavior under extreme atypical budgets
also requires further scrutiny. Future extensions could
incorporate stochastic settings, large-scale urban networks,
and advanced representations such as graph neural networks
or attention mechanisms to handle complex spatiotemporal
dynamics.
In summary, CRL-SP strikes a balance among optimality,
generalization, robustness, and efficiency, offering a scalable
solution for sustainable intelligent transportation systems. Its
broader implications lie in advancing AI from micro-level
path optimization to macro-level carbon neutrality transitions, fostering innovations in multimodal or high-uncertainty
scenarios to support global green transportation governance.
