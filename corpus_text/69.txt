Abstract—This article studies reliable shortest path (RSP) problems in stochastic transportation networks. The term
reliability in the RSP literature has many definitions, e.g., 1) maximal stochastic on-time arrival probability, 2) minimal travel time with a high-confidence constraint, 3) minimal mean and standard deviation combination, and 4)
minimal expected disutility. To the best of our knowledge, almost all state-of-the-art RSP solutions are designed to
target one specific RSP objective, and it is very difficult, if not impossible, to adapt them to other RSP objectives. To
bridge the gap, this article develops a distributional reinforcement learning (DRL)-based algorithm, namely, DRLRouter, which serves as a universal solution to the four aforementioned RSP problems. DRL-Router employs the DRL
method to approximate the full travel time distribution of a given routing policy and then makes improvements with
respect to the user-defined RSP objective through a generalized policy iteration scheme. DRL-Router is 1) universal,
i.e., it is applicable to a variety of RSP objectives; 2) model free, i.e., it does not rely on well calibrated travel time
distribution models; 3) it is adaptive with navigation objective changes; and 4) fast, i.e., it performs real-time decision making. Extensive experimental results and comparisons with baseline algorithms in various transportation
networks justify both the accuracy and efficiency of DRL-Router.

Here, we wish to articulate that, to the best of our
knowledge, almost all state-of-the-art RSP planning methodologies require at least the first-order and second-order
statistic information of the transportation network’s travel
time to be available. Meanwhile, it is very difficult, if not
impossible, for a specific RSP planning algorithm to apply
to all RSP objectives. For example, methods targeting the
SOTA problem cannot be easily adapted to solve the a- RSP
problem in a straightforward manner. However, in reality,
users may dictate their path planning objectives at will and
might shift them during runtime. For example, a traveler
wants to catch up a flight, and his or her original objective
is to arrive at the airport before 5:30 p.m. (the SOTA objective). During navigation, he or she finds that the flight’s
departure time is postponed, and in this case, he or she
prefers to travel along the cheapest path with high confidence (the a- RSP objective). Therefore, it is desirable to
design a “universal” and “adaptive” RSP algorithm that
fits a variety of RSP objectives and adapts smoothly to a
changed RSP objective.
To this end, this article proposes a distributional reinforcement learning (DRL)-based method for RSP routing
(named DRL-Router). When compared with canonical RSP
planners, DRL-Router possesses the following characteristics: it is 1) “universal,” i.e., it applies to all the aforementioned RSP planning objectives; 2) “model free,” i.e., it does
not rely on travel time distribution models; 3) “adaptive,”
i.e., it adapts with user-specific objective changes; and
4) “fast,” i.e., it makes decisions in real time (within 1 s).
Specifically, DRL-Router formulates the RSP planning
problem within the Markov decision process (MDP) framework and employs a DRL method to approximate the full
travel time distribution of a specific router; it then uses the

generalized policy iteration (GPI) method to update the
routing policy and, hence, make improvements toward the
optimal one. With the routing policy’s full travel time distribution, DRL-Router is able to calculate the user-designed
RSP objective value for each edge and then select the best one
to execute. Like canonical reinforcement learning methods,
DRL-Router also requires a prohibitive number of interactions with the environment to reach the optimal decisionmaking policy, which is undesirable. In this article, we
invent a LET path-induced warm start method to effectively
initialize the full travel time distribution estimations for all
state–action pairs and introduce an adaptive double e- greedy
exploration strategy to better guide the ego vehicle toward
the destination during the initial exploration phase. With the
LET path-induced warm start and adaptive double e- greedy
exploration strategy, DRL-Router is able to converge to the
optimal decision-making strategy efficiently. We explain the
details of the two methods in the “Methodology” section.
Here, we wish to point out that through estimating the
full travel time distribution, DRL-Router does not necessarily confine its application to the four aforementioned RSP
problems. In fact, as long as the navigation objective can be
expressed as a function of the full travel time distribution,
e.g., a conditional value at risk objective, DRL-Router can
be employed to solve the problem. This is the reason that we
characterized DRL-Router as a “universal” RSP solution. The
contributions of the article are twofold. First, from the transportation application’s perspective, 1) we propose a universal RSP path planner, i.e., DRL-Router, which applies to four
completely different RSP objectives; 2) DRL-Router adapts automatically with user-specific RSP objective changes; 3) once
training is finished, DRL-Router’s decision-making time is
within milliseconds for large-scale transportation networks;
and 4) DRL-Router is model free and thus does not need well
calibrated travel time distribution parameters as inputs. Second, from the technology innovation perspective, 1) we introduce a LET path-induced warm start method to effectively

Different from the canonical reinforcement learning algorithms, which evaluate the expected value of the
return under a given policy r, i.e., V r (s), Q r (s, a), DRL
proposes a distributional Bellman equation and targets
estimating the full distribution of a given policy’s return,
i.e., Z r (s), Z r (s, a). The distributional Bellman equation
is represented as follows:
Z r (s k, a k) = rk + 1 + cZ r (s k + 1, a k + 1). (5)
The equal sign in (5) requires that the two distributions
are “equivalent” to each other. In DRL, the Wasserstein
distance [66] is usually used to describe the distance between the two distributions. In implementation, Z r (s, a)
is, in most cases, approximated by a multinomial distribution with a prefixed set of intervals [61], [63]. The objective
is to find the best parameter configurations to minimize
the Wasserstein distance between the two distributions,
and the authors of [61] prove that (5) is a contractive operation, and hence, the related algorithms implementing (5)
converge to the optimality.

Methodology
In this section, we present DRL-Router, which serves as a
universal learning-based solution for the RSP problem. We
first reformulate the RSP planning problem from the MDP
framework perspective, then present the two interactive
modules within DRL-Router, namely, 1) policy evaluation
and 2) policy improvement. DRL-Router’s algorithm flow
process, computational complexity, and runtime complexity
are presented and analyzed toward the end of this section.

RSP Problem Reformulation Within the MDP Framework
DRL-Router formulates the general RSP problem as a sequent ial decision-mak i ng problem w it hi n t he M DP
framework, instead of treating it as monolithic nonconvex mathematical optimization problems as presented in
(1)–(4). The key to formulate the RSP planning problem
within the MDP framework is to properly map the MDPrelated elements, e.g., X, A, R, P, and c, into the path
planning-related variables and express the RSP objective
with MDP terminologies.
The mapping process is as follows: 1) the state of the
vehicle (s t ! X ) is the vehicle’s located vertex, e.g., vertex i, and the absorbing state is vertex d; 2) the action of
the vehicle (a t ! A) is the edge that the vehicle takes,
e.g., edge ij; 3) the immediate reward rt + 1 is the sampled
travel time of edge ij, i.e., c ij; 4) the state transition func-

the find the best routing policy r ), which maximizes/minimizes the corresponding performance metric value obj. (For
SOTA, the objective is to maximize obj; for all the other RSP

Policy Evaluation for DRL-Router
The policy evaluation model in DRL-Router is to estimate
the full travel time distribution for all the state–action
pairs for a given policy r. The straightforward approach
is to run the policy for all the state–action pairs multiple
times and use the sampled return (the total travel time)
values to approximate the distribution. However, the approach is inefficient in terms of both sampling complexity
and computational complexity.
We employ the TD learning [60] scheme to estimate the
return distribution of one state–action pair from the bootstrapped distribution estimates of another state–action
pair. Note that in reinforcement learning, there are altogether three schemes to estimate the return’s distribution,
namely, DP, Monte Carlo, and TD. First, DP requires the
full travel time distribution model to be available, which
restricts its application to model-free use cases. On the
other hand, although Monte Carlo is indeed a model-free
solution, it needs to wait until the end of an episode for the
estimation’s update, which is quite inefficient. In practice,
we need an algorithm that is able to update the distribution estimates based on bootstrapped estimates from the
past. This is exactly what the TD method does for the full

LET Path-Induced Warm Start of DRL-Router
We have introduced the policy evaluation module and
policy improvement module for DRL-Router in the previous two sections, respectively. Theoretically, DRL-Router
works with random initialization of the policy and its corresponding travel time distribution Z. However, with random initialization, the ego vehicle needs quite a number
of episodes for exploration in DRL-Router’s initial phase.
Thus, it is better to endow DRL-Router with a good starting point for both the policy r and its corresponding travel
time distribution Z r .
With modern sensing and communicating technologies,
it is easy to construct a database storing real travel time
samples of all the edges in the transportation network.
Then, the sample mean of the travel time can be easily obtained. In this way, we initialize DRL-Router’s policy r as
the LET path from the given vertex to the destination vertex (d), which means that at any vertex s, policy r will select the edge that is contained in the LET path from s to d.
With the initialized policy r, we can assign Z r (s, a) as the
corresponding LET path’s travel time distribution, which
is approximated with the database of travel time samples.
The reason we select a LET path to initialize the travel
time distribution estimates is that, in reality, a LET path, in
most cases, behaves quite well for any RSP problem. Therefore, using the LET-path induced distribution as the initial
travel time distribution estimation is a very good starting
point. Moreover, calculating a LET path as well as the corresponding travel time distribution is very easy with the
A* and Dijkstra algorithms. In this case, we use the LETpath induced policy to initialize the travel time distribution
estimation and then employ the GPI scheme to gradually
update the policy toward the optimal one.

Adaptive Double e– Greedy Exploration
In the “Policy Improvement for DRL-Router” section, we
introduced the policy improvement procedure for DRLRouter. The canonical e- greedy exploration strategy is
employed for exploration. However, we observe that in the
initial training stages, with the canonical e- greedy exploration strategy, the ego vehicle tends to fall into a loop,
e.g., traveling back and forth between intermediate nodes,
which is meaningless. In view of that, we invent an adaptive double e- greedy strategy, which has e 1 probability of

selecting the LET routing policy-referred action, e 2 probability of selecting the reliability metric-guided action, and
1 - e 1 - e 2 probability of selecting the action at uniform
randomness. We set e 1 = 0.9 and e 2 = 0.05 and gradually
decrease e 1 and increase e 2 as the training episodes increase, e.g., e 1 = e 1 # 0.99 Epi, where Epi refers to the number of training episodes. Similar but twisted mechanisms
can be applied to update e 2 . We verify the effectiveness of
the adaptive double e- greedy exploration strategy in the
“Simulation Results and Comparisons” section.
