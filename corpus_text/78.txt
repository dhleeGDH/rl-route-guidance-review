A dominant baseline in practice is shortest-path routing
on road graphs, where Dijkstra-style algorithms compute
minimum-cost paths with respect to static or time-varying
edge costs [2], [3]. When real-time traffic information is
available, dynamic rerouting can reduce travel time in nonstationary networks, but purely myopic replanning may cause
oscillations, herding effects, and brittle behavior when costs
evolve endogenously with network loading and disruptions
[4]. Effective large-scale routing control must therefore jointly
account for endogenous congestion dynamics, disturbancerelated risk, and the feedback between individual decisions
and network states.
Beyond myopic replanning, recent studies have explored
game-theoretic and learning-based formulations for sequential route choice under congestion, including Markov routing
games and multi-agent reinforcement learning for decentralized adaptive navigation [5]–[7]. System-optimal routing under
uncertainty and partial compliance has also been investigated
with RL and hybrid approaches, while non-compliance effects
and scalable sequential recommendation/assignment frameworks highlight the practical challenges of robustness and
scalability [8]–[10]. These developments collectively indicate
the need for routing controllers that remain scalable on real
road graphs, operate in closed loop with traffic states, and
explicitly encode resilience under disturbances.
Motivated by these gaps, our paper studies resilient routing
control in an urban road network modeled as a directed graph

Abstract—Urban road networks frequently experience recurrent congestion and non-recurrent disruptions, where individually optimal routing can degrade system-level efficiency under
mixed compliance. Naive shortest-path guidance can be overly
myopic in coupled traffic systems. As congestion is shaped by
collective route choices, it may over-react to momentary link costs
and trigger herding-like oscillations. This motivates routing controllers with decentralized execution that scale to many vehicles
while better accounting for congestion externalities. We propose
a routing framework centered on Traffic Cost Fields (TCF),
which maintain factorized edge-wise signals as an interpretable
interface between traffic states and routing decisions. A sharedparameter multi-agent actor–critic policy is trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to make periodic intersectionlevel next-hop decisions from masked local observations, enabling
decentralized execution at scale. Experiments on an abstracted
road graph extracted from Shenzhen show improved average and
tail travel-time performance over shortest-path baselines across
multiple demand levels.
Index Terms—Routing control, Transportation, Reinforcement
learning, Path planning

The following assumptions are adopted:
• The edge-level traffic states required to construct ze (t) are
available to the controller via infrastructure-side sensing
or estimation.
• Routing decisions are made at intersections. Within each

Stability under Low Penetration Rates: In lowpenetration settings (e.g.,𭟋 = 5%), controllable agents represent only a small fraction of total traffic. In such cases, stability
of the closed-loop routing process becomes critical. Because
the majority of vehicles remain uncontrolled, the routing
controller must avoid inducing excessive flow concentration
or oscillatory shifts in response to transient cost fluctuations.
The proposed TCF factorization and marginal-cost shaping are
designed to mitigate such instability by smoothing congestion
amplification and internalizing externalities locally, thereby
promoting stable decentralized adjustments rather than abrupt
global rerouting waves.
III. M ETHODOLOGY
This section presents the proposed approach for networklevel routing control on urban road graphs. We adopt a
closed-loop routing scheme over periodic decision epochs
with an initial warm-up stage of duration Twarm to establish
representative traffic conditions. After warm-up, at each epoch
tk = Twarm + k∆T , the controller observes the current traffic
cost fields {ce (tk )}e∈E and issues next-hop decisions for
eligible vehicles at intersections; the graph-based simulator
then advances traffic dynamics and refreshes edge-level states
and costs for the next epoch. The periodic next-hop policy is
learned within this simulator using a shared multi-agent deep
reinforcement learning controller.

A. Traffic Cost Fields
We construct a dynamic traffic cost field on the directed
road graph to map edge-level traffic states into traversal costs
used by routing decisions. For each directed edge e ∈ E, the
simulator provides an instantaneous occupancy ne (t), i.e., the
number of vehicles currently on e at time t. Given the static
edge attributes, including length ℓe , free-flow speed ve0 , freeflow travel time t0e , and nominal capacity Ce , we compute an
online cost representation by combining a speed–density based
travel-time estimate with a congestion delay term modeled by
the Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) function [11]. The resulting
edge-wise fields are updated online and queried by the routing
controller at decision epochs.

reduce exploration diversity. In practice, η is selected within
a moderate range to balance stability and responsiveness.

E. PPO Training Configuration
We train the shared actor–critic model using Proximal
Policy Optimization (PPO) under the periodic decision protocol [17]. Parameter sharing is applied across all controllable
agents to improve sample efficiency and scalability, with
decisions conditioned on each agent’s local observation. Advantages are computed using generalized advantage estimation
(GAE) [18], and the actor is updated via the clipped surrogate objective with clipping parameter ϵ. Training alternates
between collecting rollouts from the simulator with mixed
background traffic and controllable agents and performing
multiple epochs of minibatch updates to the actor and critic.
Unless explicitly varied in ablations, PPO hyperparameters
(learning rate lr, discount factor γ, GAE parameter λ, clipping
parameter ϵ, entropy regularization coefficient cent , value loss
coefficient cv , rollout horizon H, minibatch size B, and the
number of update epochs E) are held fixed across scenarios,

Rerouting (SPFWR) updates guidance at every intersection by
recomputing the shortest path using the current link travel-time
estimate as edge weights, yielding a purely myopic rerouting
strategy. Our method replaces this heuristic next-hop rule with
a learned PPO policy that maps masked local TCF features to
next-hop actions.
Relation to Traditional Dynamic Traffic Assignment
(DTA): Classical dynamic traffic assignment (DTA) methods
typically solve centralized optimization or equilibrium formulations over the entire network, often requiring iterative global
flow updates and network-wide fixed-point computations. Such
approaches may become computationally expensive in largescale networks or under rapidly evolving traffic conditions.
In contrast, the proposed framework performs decentralized
next-hop decisions with shared parameters and masked local
observations. Instead of solving a network-wide assignment
problem at each iteration, TCF-DRL learns closed-loop adaptation directly through interaction with traffic dynamics. While
SPFWR can be viewed as a reactive myopic approximation
to dynamic assignment, the proposed MARL-based controller
internalizes congestion externalities through reward shaping
and factorized cost fields, offering improved scalability and
adaptability in large urban networks.

D. Stability under Low Compliance
To further interpret the behavior under 5%, we analyze
qualitative stability characteristics of the learned controller.
Unlike SPFWR, which recomputes shortest paths myopically
at each intersection and may trigger synchronized rerouting
waves, TCF-DRL operates through masked next-hop decisions
using factorized local cost signals.
The capped BPR delay term and occupancy-based marginal
shaping prevent abrupt cost discontinuities, thereby reducing the likelihood of large-scale oscillations. Empirically,
Fig. 3 shows monotonic training convergence across demand
regimes, and Table II indicates no divergence in oversaturation
ratios. These observations suggest that the closed-loop routing
dynamics remain stable even when controllable agents represent only a small fraction of total traffic.
E. Reward Trends
Fig. 3 illustrates training dynamics under three background
demand levels. Across all regimes, the fitted return curves
increase and then stabilize, suggesting that PPO optimization
remains numerically stable under the proposed TCF-based
shaping.
VI. C ONCLUSIONS
This paper proposed a Traffic Cost Field (TCF)-centric
closed-loop routing framework for mixed urban traffic
networks, where controllable vehicles perform periodic
intersection-level next-hop decisions after a warm-up stage.
By factorizing edge-level travel time, nonlinear congestion
delay, and disturbance risk into interpretable traffic fields,
and training a shared-parameter PPO-based multi-agent actor–critic controller over masked local observations, the proposed method enables scalable decentralized execution on realworld road graphs.
