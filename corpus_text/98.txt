Disaster-Aware Path Planning Based on
Reinforcement Learning for Postearthquake
Emergency Response
Jianchong Guo , Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Yuting Wan , Member, IEEE,
Ailong Ma , Senior Member, IEEE, and Yanfei Zhong , Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract— After earthquake disasters, ensuring that emergency rescue operations reach affected areas as quickly as
possible, to maximize the rescue of lives and property and
minimize secondary disaster losses, is the primary task of
postearthquake emergency response. Remote sensing technology,
characterized by its large-scale coverage, noncontact nature,
and rapid response capabilities, provides valuable information
for disaster area assessment and postearthquake emergency
response path planning. However, existing postdisaster emergency
path planning studies often fail to utilize this rich information.
Traditional path planning algorithms insufficiently consider the
disaster situation, hindering the efficient utilization of rescue
forces and resources. To address these challenges, this study
proposes a disaster-aware path planning method based on
reinforcement learning for postearthquake emergency response
(P2DARL). The P2DARL method utilizes real disaster information provided by high-resolution remote sensing images and
models it in a reinforcement learning environment. This allows
the intelligent agent to learn optimal postearthquake emergency
response path planning strategies through continuous trial-anderror interactions with the environment. This approach facilitates
the optimal allocation of rescue resources and enhances rescue
efficiency. Experiments using real earthquake disaster images
demonstrate that the P2DARL method effectively plans paths
that cover more affected centers in mid-short distance scenarios,
maximizing rescue efficiency and significantly reducing casualties
and economic losses resulting from earthquake disasters.
Index Terms— Disaster-aware path planning, high-resolution
remote sensing, postearthquake emergency response, reinforcement learning.

Despite advancements in damage assessment, integrating
this rich information into effective path planning remains a
critical challenge. Emergency response path planning systems
can be broadly classified into traditional algorithms and modern approaches. Traditional path planning algorithms, such as
A* [9] and D* [10] methods, typically convert the application environment into a discrete space, allowing movement
between discrete states. These algorithms are widely used
due to their simplicity and effectiveness, but they are prone
to falling into local optima. Modern methods include evolutionary computation and reinforcement learning approaches.
Evolutionary computation methods, such as genetic algorithms
(GAs) [11], [12], particle swarm optimization (PSO) [13], and
ant colony optimization (ACO) [14], seek optimal solutions
by iteratively optimizing a population of candidate solutions.
However, these methods often suffer from relatively low
computational efficiency. On the other hand, reinforcement
learning methods have been extensively studied in the field
of path planning, focusing primarily on online path planning
and multiobjective path planning [15], [16]. Reinforcement
learning-based online path planning involves making real-time
path planning decisions based on the current environmental
information perceived by the agent. This approach is commonly applied in dynamically changing environments, such as
autonomous driving [17], [18]. Multiobjective path planning
with reinforcement learning considers not only the path length
but also other benefits as decision-making criteria, leveraging the trial-and-error learning characteristic of reinforcement
learning to find globally optimal solutions. Reinforcement
learning methods are well-suited for addressing multiobjective
path planning problems and have the potential for real-time
planning, making them highly applicable to postearthquake
emergency response tasks.
In actual postearthquake emergency path planning tasks,
existing research primarily treats them as a special case of the

vehicle routing problem (VRP) [19], studied within simulated
postdisaster environments. These studies mainly focus on
assumptions about disaster scenarios and the formulation of
objective functions, followed by the application of traditional
algorithms to find optimal paths. While some progress has
been made, several issues remain as follows.
1) Overly Idealized Environmental Conditions: Current
methods often lack the utilization of the rich information provided by remote sensing technology and
typically simulate disaster scenarios in overly idealized
environments [20], [21]. These simulations frequently
make assumptions such as direct connections between
locations and favorable traffic conditions, which do not
reflect the complexity and variability of real postdisaster
situations [22].
2) Insufficient Consideration of Disaster Impact: The
formulation of objective functions in many studies primarily focuses on factors such as rescue path distance
and time, while neglecting specific disaster conditions [23], [24]. Effective postearthquake emergency
response should consider the severity of damage, the
number of victims, and the specific needs of affected
areas. This requires a more nuanced approach to planning and deploying rescue efforts that are tailored to the
varying degrees of impact and urgency across different
disaster scenarios.
To address the aforementioned critical issues, this article
proposes a disaster-aware path planning method based on reinforcement learning for postearthquake emergency response,
called P2DARL. This method utilizes HRI to obtain interpreted information and employs reinforcement learning to
incorporate disaster conditions into the emergency path planning process. The main contributions of this article are as
follows.
1) A Framework for Postearthquake Emergency Response
Path Planning: By modeling postearthquake emergency
response path planning as a reinforcement learning task

II. R ELATED R ESEARCH BACKGROUND
In the proposed method, we utilize reinforcement learning
to tackle the challenge of disaster-aware path planning in
postearthquake emergency response. Following this, we will
present the fundamental theory of reinforcement learning and
discuss its application in path planning tasks.
A. Reinforcement Learning
A reinforcement learning problem can be represented as
a system consisting of an agent and an environment [25],
as shown in Fig. 2. To achieve a specific goal or task, the
agent first interacts with the environment to obtain the current
state. The agent then observes this state, denoted as st , and
uses the information to select an action at . The environment
receives the action at , transitions to the next state st+1 , and
provides the new state and a reward rt to the agent, completing
one time step. This process of observing the state, taking an
action, and receiving a reward repeats until the environment
terminates, which may occur when the goal is achieved or the
task is completed.
Reinforcement learning tasks are commonly described using
a Markov decision process (MDP), which is represented by
the tuple (S, A, P(·), R(·)). The components of the MDP are
defined as follows.
1) S is the set of states, where st ∈ S represents the state
at time t.

planning tasks. As a result, we will not delve into the specifics
of reinforcement learning for online path planning.

III. P ROPOSED P2DARL M ETHOD
To effectively plan emergency response paths following
an earthquake, two main tasks must be accomplished. First,
it is crucial to accurately assess the postearthquake disaster
conditions. Second, based on this assessment, targeted path
planning must be performed, taking into account the affected
areas to efficiently allocate rescue forces and resources. This
approach aims to maximize rescue efficiency while minimizing
casualties and economic losses caused by the earthquake.
The general framework of the proposed P2DARL method,

as illustrated in Fig. 3, includes three main steps: 1) disaster
information extraction; 2) postearthquake environmental modeling; and 3) disaster-aware path planning agent.
A. Disaster Information Extraction
To facilitate postearthquake emergency response path planning, it is essential to understand both the disaster situation
and the damage to the road network. The P2DARL framework leverages dual-temporal HRI as input. First, it uses the
ChangeOS framework, which is based on deep object semantic
change detection, to extract and assess postearthquake building
damage and identify core affected areas [32]. Second, the
D-LinkNet is employed to extract road network information
from postearthquake imagery for planning emergency rescue paths [33]. Following this, brief descriptions of these
postearthquake remote sensing image interpretation methods
will be provided.
1) Building Damage Assessment Using HRI: Sudden natural and man-made disasters pose significant threats to human
life and property, making the automated assessment of postdisaster damage crucial for many countries. The U.S. Defense
Innovation Unit (DIU), in collaboration with humanitarian
aid and disaster recovery organizations, launched the xView2
Challenge. This competition utilizes the xBD dataset, a labeled
dual-temporal HRI dataset [34]. The xBD dataset is distinguished by its diverse range of natural disasters and extensive
spatiotemporal coverage, providing robust generalization for
postearthquake scenarios. It categorizes building damage into
four levels: no damage, minor damage, major damage, and
destroyed, with the specific assessment criteria illustrated in
Fig. 4.
Given the high demands for timeliness in postdisaster
emergency response, we use ChangeOS, a top-performing
model from the xView2 Challenge, to assess postearthquake
building damage. ChangeOS framework models the building
damage assessment task as a bi-temporal change detection

2) Postearthquake Road Extraction Using HRI: Road
extraction is a fundamental task in the field of remote sensing [35]. In postearthquake scenarios, roads are often disrupted
by earthquakes and landslides. Additionally, in nonurban areas,
road network data may not have been updated for a long
time, making existing data difficult to apply directly for
postearthquake emergency response path planning. This delay
can hinder rescue operations and risk missing the crucial 72-h
rescue window. Therefore, rapidly obtaining postearthquake
road network data is a critical step in facilitating efficient
rescue efforts.
In disaster-stricken areas, especially in developing countries,
the lack of up-to-date maps and obstacle information greatly
impacts postdisaster emergency response. To address this, the
CVPR DeepGlobe 2018 Road Extraction Challenge was introduced by DeepGlobe [36]. This study employs the winning
solution from the challenge, D-LinkNet, for postearthquake
road extraction. The structure of the D-LinkNet is illustrated
in Fig. 5(b). The D-LinkNet retains detailed feature layer
information while expanding the receptive field in the central part and integrating multiscale features. This approach
effectively handles the narrowness, connectivity, complexity,
and long span of roads, resulting in improved road extraction
accuracy.
B. Postearthquake Environmental Modeling
Using reinforcement learning for postearthquake emergency
path planning requires modeling the postdisaster environment
to create an interactive setting for the agent. Previously,
we obtained postearthquake building damage assessments
and road extraction results. Based on these assessments, we

5633314

consider modeling the postdisaster environment as either a
raster or a vector environment.
First, in the raster environment, the original interpreted
information can be effectively converted since the imagery
itself is raster data. Constructing a reinforcement learning
environment in this format is straightforward, with the action
space simply comprising the eight neighboring pixels. However, several issues arise as follows.
1) HRI is massive, occupying significant memory. Typically, deep learning models use a sliding window to crop
high-resolution imagery for interpretation, followed by
reassembly. However, path planning requires consideration of global information, not just local details.
2) Path planning is essentially a graph search problem. Unlike traditional robot grid path planning,
postearthquake emergency path planning does not
require pixel-level obstacle avoidance. For example,
on a specific road grid, the agent does not need
to make pixel-level decisions but simply follows the
road. The critical aspect is planning transitions at road
intersections.
3) Storing data at the grid level leads to considerable redundancy. As illustrated in Fig. 5(b), the road extraction
results occupy a small fraction of a high-resolution
postearthquake urban image, while the disaster centers
are represented as points. Thus, using the original raster
for environment modeling is not ideal.
However, using vector data for postearthquake emergency
path planning presents challenges in data conversion but effectively addresses issues encountered with raster-based planning.
Therefore, this study explores postearthquake emergency path
planning based on vector data.
1) Postprocessing of Postearthquake Building Damage
Assessment: Using dual-temporal remote sensing imagery as
input to ChangeOS for building damage assessment, postprocessing of the assessment results is necessary to identify
disaster centers or areas with severe building damage.
Kernel density analysis is a method to calculate the density of features within their surrounding neighborhoods. It is
commonly used in community planning to analyze housing
density or crime incidents [37]. In the context of postdisaster
scenarios, disaster centers can be understood as peak points
in kernel density analysis, representing areas with the most
severe building damage.
This study begins by masking the building damage assessment results, setting masks for undamaged buildings and
nonbuilding areas. It then performs a raster-to-point conversion, assigning values of 0.5 for undamaged buildings, 1 for
minor damaged buildings, 2 for major damaged buildings,
and 3 for destroyed buildings, allowing for subsequent kernel
density analysis operations.
For point features, kernel density analysis calculates the
density of point features around each output raster cell.
Conceptually, each point feature is covered by a smooth
surface, with the highest surface value directly over the point
location. This value gradually decreases with distance from
the point until it reaches zero at the search radius distance.

derived from postearthquake remote sensing imagery often
includes many dead ends due to road fractures. Since the agent
may not have a complete understanding of the environment’s
state, a negative reward is introduced to discourage the agent
from moving toward these dead ends. Similar to the reward
for reaching the target point, rdead is a large negative reward.
If the number of connected neighbors in the agent’s eightneighborhood is 1, J is set to 1. This negative reward,
as defined in (1), continuously influences the state leading to
the dead end, thereby decreasing the likelihood of the agent
moving toward dead ends in future actions.

E. Limitations in Real-World Applications
Despite the promising performance of our proposed method
in short to medium-distance path planning, several limitations
warrant consideration as follows.
1) Path Planning Challenges in Long-Distance Scenarios:
The reinforcement learning approach faces efficiency
challenges in long-distance route planning. Due to
the exploration–exploitation tradeoff in the ϵ-greedy
algorithm, the probability of discovering optimal paths
decreases exponentially with the number of decision
points. This makes the method less effective for
extended routes where multiple sequential optimal decisions are required.
2) Reward Function Limitations in Fragmented Road Networks: The reward function design is challenged by
fragmented road networks in postdisaster scenarios.
When continuous roads are interpreted as disconnected
segments, the search areas for disaster centers become
inconsistent. As illustrated in Fig. 14, the fragmentation
of a road length a into segments b and c results in
reduced search coverage areas, affecting the accuracy
of proximity-based reward calculations.
3) Temporal and State Representation Challenges: The
current state representation methods have limitations
in capturing the complex characteristics of disasteraffected areas. While adapted from urban environments,
these methods struggle with the irregular and incomplete
nature of postdisaster road networks. This constrains the
development of an online-capable path-aware agent that
can effectively respond to evolving disaster scenarios.

V. C ONCLUSION
This article investigates postearthquake emergency path
planning using dual-phase high-resolution remote sensing
images. By leveraging these images for detailed interpretation,
the research models the interpreted data as a reinforcement
learning environment. The proposed method integrates both
path length and proximity to disaster centers into the path
planning process, allowing for a tradeoff between minimizing
path length and maximizing coverage of disaster centers.
The experimental results, based on real earthquake disaster
images, demonstrate that the proposed method effectively
balances path length and disaster coverage in medium and
short path planning scenarios. While the approach enables
efficient deployment of rescue forces along optimized paths,
challenges remain in long-distance planning and fragmented
road network scenarios. Future work could focus on developing more robust state representation methods for disaster
environments and improving the handling of fragmented road
networks, ultimately enhancing the practical applicability of
the proposed approach.
