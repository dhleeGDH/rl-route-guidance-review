Abstract—Urban route planning is a critical challenge for
Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) due to the high variability of traffic
conditions and the topological complexity of road networks. Traditional routing systems often rely on static maps or algorithms
that do not account for the temporal evolution of traffic, resulting
in suboptimal solutions in real-world scenarios. This work proposes a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) based framework
for optimal path generation in realistic urban environments,
leveraging historical traffic data provided by TomTom APIs. The
road network of the city of Bari is used as a case study, and
traffic conditions are modeled across three distinct time slots to
capture typical daily traffic fluctuations. The routing problem
is formally modeled as a Markov Decision Process, where the
agent interacts with a dynamic environment and learns a policy
that minimizes travel time and avoids congestion. The agent
is trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization algorithm, a
state-of-the-art actor-critic method that ensures stable learning
in complex environments. The simulation environment is built
using Simulation of Urban MObility, with traffic flows generated
coherently with the data collected from TomTom. Experimental
results show that the agent trained with time-dependent traffic
information outperforms static-data-based strategies, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed approach for intelligent
and adaptive transportation systems.
Index Terms—Deep Reinforcement Learning, Vehicle Routing,
Autonomous Vehicles, Traffic Simulation, Intelligent Transportation Systems.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Autonomous driving represents one of the most transformative and ambitious challenges of modern engineering, with the
potential to redefine urban transportation, significantly enhance
road safety, and mitigate the environmental impact caused
by excessive traffic emissions. Autonomous vehicles (AVs)
are not merely a technological innovation but a fundamental
component in the development of sustainable urban mobility
systems [1]. As urban populations expand and congestion
worsens, the paradigm of smart cities has gained prominence.
In such cities, advanced technologies are employed to manage
resources efficiently, optimize urban services, and improve the
quality of life for citizens [2].
Smart mobility constitutes a key pillar of this paradigm,
aiming to streamline traffic flow, minimize travel times, and

the problem of efficient route planning. Traditional algorithms
such as Dijkstra and A* have served as the backbone of

characterized by dynamic, unpredictable traffic conditions [4].
These methods also struggle to adapt in real-time and to scale

To address these limitations, Deep Reinforcement Learning
(DRL) has emerged as a powerful methodology. DRL techniques enable systems to learn optimal routing strategies by
interacting with both simulated and real environments, offering
robust adaptability in the face of stochastic and variable traffic
conditions, demonstrated superior performance in learning and
adapting to complex routing challenges where conventional
algorithms fail [5]. Notably, DRL has been applied to traffic

potential within intelligent transportation systems [6].
Nonetheless, existing DRL-based path planning methods often neglect real-time traffic dynamics or rely on oversimplified
traffic models, which limits their applicability in realistic smart
city contexts [7]. While efforts have been made to integrate
traffic prediction into routing, such as deep clustered convolutional networks for traffic state prediction [8], comprehensive
frameworks that unify route planning with dynamic traffic
conditions remain scarce.
This paper proposes an advanced DRL-based route planning
framework for AVs that explicitly incorporates real-time traffic
conditions in an approach based on the systems of systems
paradigm. The originality of this contribution lies in several
key aspects: (i) traffic scenarios are modeled with high fidelity
using the Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO) tool [9];
(ii) real-time traffic data is integrated through the TomTom
APIs [10], allowing accurate representation of current road
conditions; (iii) the urban road network is systematically
partitioned into geographic clusters, enabling scalable and
efficient route computation; (iv) traffic patterns are captured
using time-slot segmentation, allowing the model to anticipate
and adapt to temporal variations in traffic flow. The integration of simulation with Artificial Intelligence tools enables
the proposed framework to achieve enhanced adaptability to
dynamic traffic conditions, improved scalability for large urban

II. R ELATED W ORK
Route planning represents a fundamental problem across
multiple domains, including robotics, autonomous driving, and
aerial mobility, and has thus become a cornerstone of intelligent transportation systems and smart city development. In the
context of aerial navigation, for instance, DRL-based planners
have demonstrated robustness both in simulation and in realworld Unmanned Aerial Vehicle flight tests, confirming their
applicability in highly dynamic and uncertain environments
[11]. Similarly, in safety-critical driving scenarios, hybrid
solutions that combine trajectory prediction via graph-based
models with model predictive control have proven effective in
ensuring both adaptability and safety [12]. These contributions
highlight the versatility of DRL-based route planning across
heterogeneous application domains.
Building upon this general foundation, research has progressively focused on ground vehicles and autonomous mobility.
Early works emphasized the role of reward design in improving DRL-based path planning, enabling smoother and more
efficient trajectories compared to classical algorithms [13].
Subsequent hybrid approaches integrated DRL with potential
field methods or model-based local planners such as Dynamic
Window Approach, thereby enhancing convergence, obstacle
avoidance, and real-world applicability [14], [15]. These studies demonstrated that combining learning-based and classical
paradigms can yield effective solutions for small-scale motion
planning tasks.
The research focus has since shifted toward large-scale vehicular routing. DRL has been framed as a sequential decisionmaking process for dynamic traffic networks, where simulation
environments such as SUMO have provided realistic testbeds
to validate adaptive routing strategies [16]. These works consistently showed that DRL-based routing policies outperform
traditional optimization baselines under congested and nonstationary conditions. More advanced approaches addressed
scalability issues through hierarchical architectures enriched
with transformer-based attention mechanisms, allowing realtime routing decisions even in dense metropolitan environments with numerous agents and fluctuating traffic demands
[17].
Parallel to these developments, attention has been devoted
to enriching the objectives of route planning. Bi-criteria frameworks have shown how DRL can balance travel efficiency with

additional dimensions such as safety or environmental sustainability [18]. Other works introduced context-aware routing
models, where external factors such as demand patterns or
user preferences are explicitly incorporated into the decisionmaking process [19]. Moreover, the integration of graph neural
networks with DRL has enabled the representation of complex traffic interactions, while parallel simulation approaches
have improved scalability, allowing policies to approximate
Dynamic User Equilibrium solutions in realistic traffic regimes
[20].
Within this context, the framework proposed in this paper
extends the state of the art by explicitly integrating real-world
traffic data with DRL-based path planning in a high-fidelity
simulation environment. Unlike prior approaches that rely
primarily on synthetic or simulation-only traffic scenarios [16],
[17], our method incorporates historical traffic patterns via the
TomTom APIs directly into SUMO, enabling the training and
evaluation of routing policies under conditions that closely
replicate real-world dynamics. Additionally, the systematic
clustering of the urban road network and the temporal slotting
of traffic flows allow the framework to scale efficiently to large
metropolitan networks and to adapt to temporal variations in
traffic, addressing limitations in both scalability and temporal
awareness present in existing methods [17], [20]. These enhancements collectively support the development of adaptive
and deployable routing strategies suitable for next-generation
smart city applications.
III. P ROBLEM F ORMULATION
Efficient route planning for AVs in dynamic urban environments is inherently a sequential decision-making problem,
where the agent must select optimal actions (routes) while
adapting to stochastic and time-varying traffic conditions. This
process is best modeled as a Markov Decision Process, which
provides a mathematical framework for modeling decisionmaking in environments where outcomes are partly random
and partly under the control of the agent.
The urban road network is modeled as a directed graph
G = (V, E), where V represents intersections or waypoints
and E denotes road segments.
Fig. 1 shows an example traffic scenario used for route
planning.

by the tuple (S, A, T , R, γ), where:
• S (State space): encodes the current location of the
vehicle, the current traffic slot, and the normalized time
slot to represent the vehicle’s temporal and spatial context
in the network;
• A (Action space): representing the set of possible outgoing edges (roads) that the vehicle can choose from at its
current location;
• T (Transition probability function): models the state
evolution driven by the selected route and real-time traffic
conditions simulated in SUMO;

IV. D EEP R EINFORCEMENT L EARNING F RAMEWORK
To address the route planning problem under dynamic traffic
conditions, a DRL framework is proposed, combining an actorcritic structure with traffic-aware decision-making.
A. Actor-Critic Framework

D. Transition Function
The transition from state s(t) to next state s′ = s(t + 1)
depends on:
• the action a(t), which determines the next edge e(t + 1);
• the traffic dynamics, influenced by time slots and SUMO
simulation behavior.
Thus, T (s′ |s, a) is not explicitly known but is simulated

The DRL model adopts an Actor-Critic Architecture [21],
composed of two neural networks:
• Actor network: maps states to actions using a probabilistic policy π(a|s). It outputs the probability of taking
each possible action in a given state;
• Critic network: estimates the value of a state or the
advantage of an action using the value function V (s) or
Q(s, a).

The Actor and Critic work synergistically: the Actor selects
actions based on its policy, while the Critic evaluates those
actions using an estimate of the advantage function. The
advantage function A(s, a) is defined as:
A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V (s)
where Q(s, a) is the action-value function and V (s) is the
state-value function. This difference quantifies how much
better an action is compared to the average action in that state.
The advantage function is closely related to the Temporal
Difference (TD) error, computed as:
δ = r + γV (s′ ) − V (s)
where r is the reward, γ the discount factor, and s, s′ are the
current and next states, respectively.

The city of Bari was selected as the testbed due to its diverse
traffic conditions, ranging from low-congestion residential
zones to highly congested urban centers. To ensure realistic
simulation, the urban map was extracted from OpenStreetMap
[24] and converted for use in SUMO. Historical and real-time
traffic data were retrieved from TomTom APIs, providing a
reliable basis for modeling traffic dynamics.
A critical feature of this work is the partitioning of the
Bari road network into geographic clusters using K-means
clustering. This clustering approach enables scalable simulation by managing traffic flows at the cluster level rather than
at the individual road segment level, significantly reducing
computational complexity during training while preserving
traffic realism.
The DRL agent, implemented using Ray RLlib [25] with
PPO, was trained to optimize routes that balance path efficiency with traffic avoidance. The simulation environment
integrates SUMO for traffic modeling, TraCI for real-time
control, and Ray RLlib for DRL training, creating a cohesive
platform for evaluating route planning strategies.

C. Traffic-Aware Routing
The framework integrates real traffic data retrieved from
TomTom APIs, aggregated into three traffic slots: morning,
afternoon, and evening. Each slot reflects distinct congestion
patterns and is used to simulate traffic dynamics in SUMO.
The agent observes the current traffic slot and normalized time,
allowing it to adapt its routing policy to temporal variations
in traffic. Additionally, the urban map is partitioned into
geographic clusters using K-means [23], facilitating scalable
simulation and efficient policy learning. By combining actorcritic DRL with real-time traffic modeling and clustering-based
partitioning, the proposed framework enables dynamic and
traffic-aware route planning for autonomous vehicles.

B. Map Clustering
To mitigate computational complexity, the Bari road network was partitioned into geographic clusters using the Kmeans algorithm. This approach renders the routing and traffic
flow management problem more tractable by decomposing the
network into smaller, localized sub-regions.
Within each cluster, ingress and egress points were explicitly
defined, allowing traffic flows to be generated at the cluster
level rather than across the entire network. Junctions were
assigned to nclusters (set to 7, reflecting the spatial extent and
complexity of the Bari road network represented) based on
proximity to cluster centroids, and edges were subsequently
organized according to their cluster membership, with relevant
information on allowed and restricted vehicle types systematically recorded. Essential data, including cluster centroids, all
edges, internal border edges, and complete boundary edges,
were extracted and stored to facilitate modular traffic assignment.
Such an organization markedly enhances scalability: rather
than addressing a combinatorial explosion of potential routes
across the entire urban network, the framework only needs to
manage flows within and between clusters. Consequently, the
number of candidate routes is reduced, and traffic data can
be allocated in a modular manner. Each cluster was assigned
specific traffic volumes derived from TomTom density indices,
thereby preserving heterogeneity in the simulation.
Overall, clustering transforms the routing problem from
a global, computationally intensive task into a modular and
scalable framework, enabling traffic flows and route planning
to be managed at multiple levels of spatial granularity.

C. Training Process
The DRL agent was trained using Python on an 11th Gen
Intel Core i7-11370H CPU (3.30 GHz) with 16 GB of RAM.
Due to the constrained computational resources available in a
home setup, the training process was limited to 260 episodes.
This choice was driven by the high computational cost resulting from the joint execution of SUMO traffic simulation and
the reinforcement learning algorithm.
To optimize resource usage and reduce training time, SUMO
was executed in non-GUI mode, leveraging a customizable
option provided within the developed code framework. This
setup allowed for headless simulation runs, reducing graphical
overhead and enabling more efficient use of available computation capacity.

VI. C ONCLUSION
This paper presents a traffic-aware route planning approach
based on DRL, evaluated in a realistic urban environment. The
experimental results show that the DRL agent achieves significant improvements over conventional routing strategies, such
as those provided by Google Maps. In particular, the agent
demonstrates the ability to anticipate and avoid congested
segments, opting for alternative routes that reduce exposure
to traffic without compromising arrival success.
The comparison highlights how learning-based routing can
surpass static or myopic heuristics by incorporating long-term
traffic dynamics into decision-making. These findings support
the practical potential of intelligent routing frameworks for
future autonomous mobility systems and urban traffic management solutions.
Future work will focus on refining map accuracy, improving
computational efficiency, and expanding the framework to
larger urban networks, further enhancing its scalability and
practical deployment potential in smart city environments.
