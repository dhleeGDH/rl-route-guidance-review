ABSTRACT
Traffic management has become a major issue in urban planning due to the increasing number of vehicles on urban roads.
In this study, we introduce a novel approach using the Reinforcement Learning (RL) technique to address the vehicle routing
problem (VRP). We explored the effectiveness of Double Q-Learning enhanced by Prioritized Experience Replay (DQL-PER)
in optimizing vehicle routing to shorten travel times and reduce congestion. Using the Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO),
this method manipulates traffic flow during peak hours to improve urban mobility. DQL-PER stands out due to its superior
performance in managing complex traffic systems characterized by multiple interconnected variables and dynamic conditions
inherent in urban traffic networks. Compared to standard Q-learning, DQL-PER reduces overestimation bias and facilitates
faster convergence toward optimal solutions. This paper includes a comparison between DQL-PER and other RL methods,
namely Q-learning, Double Q-learning (DQL), and deep Q-network (DQN), demonstrating its benefits through simulations
and analysis. We also perform a scalability analysis to evaluate the algorithm’s performance across network sizes, with node
counts 𝑁 = 39, 545, 1672, 3236, and 9652, showing that DQL-PER performs exhaustively over larger networks, demonstrating its
scalability potential. DQL-PER offers a scalable solution with the potential to transform urban transportation systems.

Introduction

Traffic congestion is an increasing problem in urban areas worldwide that results in longer drive times, higher CO2 emissions, and
more stress for drivers. With the increasing number of vehicles
on the road, the need for effective traffic management systems
also increases. According to [1] report of 2023 from INRIX, the
average U.S. person spent 51 h sitting in 2022–15 h more than
in 2021, though it remains 50% lower than the pre-pandemic
high of 99 h in 2019. However, the biggest challenge for traffic
management authorities is optimizing routes with less traffic
or congestion. The problem is popularly known as the ‘vehicle
routing problem’ (VRP), which searches for the most efficient
path for a vehicle to deliver goods to different locations (Figure 1).
When applied to urban mobility, the goal of VRP becomes a

search for ways to reduce travel time and avoid congestion
in a stochastic environment. Traditionally, traffic management
strategies have relied on static path planning, using a collection
of well-reputed algorithms. Classic graph-based methods were
the foundation of such planning; Dijkstra’s algorithm [2] is wellknown for finding the shortest path in a static network. The
A-star (A*) algorithm [3] adds heuristics to speed up the search
process. In addition, studies have been done to use naturebased algorithms, such as ant colony optimization (ACO) [4]
and particle swarm optimization (PSO) [5], which mimic natural
processes to solve complex routing problems. Genetic algorithms
[6] use evolutionary strategies to create optimal routes over
time, while sample-based planning techniques, such as rapidly
exploring random tree (RRT) [7] and its variants, randomly
explore the search space and are useful for path planning for

flexibility to respond efficiently to stochastic patterns in traffic. To
handle this, dynamic route planning [8] integrates methods that
can respond to traffic information dynamically, such as adaptive
traffic control systems, which modify signal timings based on
live traffic conditions, and dynamic routing algorithms, which
integrate incoming traffic data to update and optimize routes.
Advancements in innovations progressed and made use of traffic
prediction models with machine learning (ML) for traffic pattern
recognition [9]. This paved the way for better traffic management
systems. Even with the advances in dynamic routing, many
of these algorithms struggle with the scaling of urban road
networks. As a city network grows transportation networks also
become larger and more complicated. Algorithms that work
well on smaller scales may not be able to handle the increased
complexity. They can become less efficient and can fail to provide
optimal routes. This is where reinforcement learning (RL) comes
into play [10, 11]. RL is a type of ML method where an agent
learns to make decisions by performing actions and recording the
results. In traffic management, an RL agent might try different
routes and learn which ones are usually the fastest or least
congested over time. The agent’s decision-making process is
modelled as a Markov decision process (MDP), a mathematical
framework that captures the probabilities and rewards of moving
from one state (or traffic condition) to another.
This paper focuses on a specific RL technique called double
Q-learning (DQL), which is enhanced by a mechanism known
as prioritized experience replay (PER). Drawing inspirations
from [12, 13] focusing on the advantages of PER and DQL, this
approach helps to overcome the limitations of vanilla RL methods
in many ways. It can handle the complex, high-dimensional
scenarios typical of urban traffic systems and learn more efficient
policies faster. To test the double Q-learning with prioritized
experience replay (DQL-PER) algorithm, we use the Simulation
of Urban MObility (SUMO) platform [14]. SUMO allows us
to create realistic traffic scenarios and simulate the behaviour
of individual drivers and vehicles. It is an invaluable tool for
researchers because it lets us test how well our algorithms might

and also conducts a comparative analysis with other established
algorithms in the field, including traditional Q-learning, double

its efficacy and efficiency in optimizing traffic routes. Additionally, we are also examining how efficiently DQL-PER can
scale with increasing network size by performing scalability

of how these methods perform as traffic networks get bigger,

Related Work

The work on vehicle routing and traffic management problems
has gone through a lot of evolution over the decades from
conventional algorithms to adopting artificial intelligence
(AI)-based models. This development showed the acceptance
of better and more dynamic real-time solutions to tackle the
increasing complexity of urban traffic systems. Initially, many
classical algorithms were proposed such as Dijkstra’s and the
A* algorithm. They provided efficient solutions for handling
the static shortest path problems. However, these solutions
had limitations in adapting to the stochastic nature of traffic
dynamics, which motivated the development of more adaptable
algorithms. Graph-based methods like the Bellman–Ford
algorithm [15] and Floyd–Warshall algorithm [16] expanded
the knowledge base for the shortest path problems to handle
more complex network structures. Other approaches involving

for models to learn and refine routing policies for problems in

that enables the handling of complex and high-dimensional state

against traditional heuristics and highlighted RL’s adaptability
for dynamic VRP scenarios. Dynamic attention model (AM-D) by
[26] proposed a dynamic attention mechanism that updates node
embeddings during solution construction for VRP. This approach
improves adaptability and generalization across varying problem

large-scale routing tasks. Another work by [28] explored graph
neural networks combined with dynamic programming to solve
VRP efficiently. Their approach achieved strong performance
on tasks like TSP and VRP while improving scalability and
solution speed. On the same lines, [29] introduced a Q-learningbased multiobjective evolutionary algorithm (QMOEA) to solve
VRP with time windows (VRPTW), optimizing vehicle duration,
energy consumption, and customer satisfaction. Their hybrid
initialization and adaptive local search strategies significantly
improved convergence and solution quality. In the same year, a
study conducted by [30] provided an extensive review of recent
advancements in solving the VRP using RL techniques. They
highlighted the benefits of RL over conventional methods like
OR-Tools and meta-heuristics, emphasizing RL’s adaptability for
dynamic and stochastic VRP variants. The work underscores
RL’s ability to efficiently handle large-scale instances while
optimizing vehicle routes, although computational challenges
remain a limitation.
In their study [31] tackled the stochastic vehicle routing problem (SVRP) by introducing a RL model that incorporates both
stochastic demands and travel costs. The proposed RL framework
outperformed state-of-the-art methods like ACO, reducing travel
costs by 3.43%, while demonstrating robustness across varying

problem sizes and stochastic settings. Recently, [32] further
advanced RL applications in routing problems by proposing
a DQN-based adaptive search to solve time-dependent green
VRPs, achieving significant improvements in solution quality
and diversity. [33] in their work, combined Q-learning with
genetic algorithms to optimize traffic light scheduling, achieving notable improvements in vehicle waiting times compared
to conventional methods. Then, [34] reviewed a great deal
of ML-based VRP methods, emphasizing RL’s capability to
handle dynamic and large-scale problems while identifying
challenges like computational cost and generalization. A wellsummarized paper by [35] provided a comprehensive survey
of ML-based approaches to solving VRPs. Their work classifies
methods into end-to-end and hybrid approaches while evaluating
state-of-the-art solutions on benchmark datasets. The study
emphasizes the potential of ML models to address complex
routing scenarios efficiently compared to traditional optimization
methods
There has been major progress in traffic simulation tools that provide better computational power and have built-in tools for route
and vehicle characterization. These tools help in improving the
simulation and evaluation of traffic scenarios. The open-source
SUMO simulation platform with its comprehensive supporting
tools and external control features via APIs, has provided a
conducive platform for applying and testing RL algorithms
for routing-related problems. Despite these advancements, the
application of RL algorithms utilizing the SUMO framework
remains an unexplored path. Research efforts by [36–38] have
shown progress in addressing various traffic navigation challenges using RL-based algorithms within the SUMO environment
by tackling problems from traffic signal prediction to traffic
controller benchmarking. A notable experiment by [39] showed
the potential of RL-based algorithms to enable vehicles to learn
better navigation strategies in a controlled setting using SUMO
and TraCI. Building on this foundation, [40] presented a dynamic
learning rate in RL for real-time route navigation, showcasing the
algorithm’s adaptability to sudden traffic changes. In this study,
we leverage the DQL-PER model to contribute towards providing
solutions to the VRP. DQL-PER is a robust RL method and its
enhanced learning mechanisms make it a promising candidate
for addressing the complexities of urban traffic management. By
prioritizing important experiences and reducing overestimation
bias, DQL-PER potentially delivers improved solutions for realtime traffic management and can address the challenges of traffic
congestion taking into account the stochastic nature of urban
traffic flows. To evaluate DQL-PER, we aim to test its efficacy
against other state-of-the-art RL algorithms namely Q-learning,
double Q-learning, and DQN.

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)

At the core of RL algorithms lies the MDP, which provides
a mathematical framework for decision-making in stochastic
environments. An MDP is defined as a tuple (𝑆, 𝐴, 𝑃, 𝑅, 𝛾), where:
∙ S: A finite set of states, 𝑆 = {𝑠1 , 𝑠2 , … , 𝑠𝑛 }. In the context of
traffic management, each state represents the current traffic
condition at a specific road segment or intersection.
∙ A: A finite set of actions, 𝐴 = {𝑎1 , 𝑎2 , … , 𝑎𝑚 }. Each action
corresponds to a possible decision, such as selecting the next
road segment for a vehicle.
∙ P: A state transition probability function, 𝑃 ∶ 𝑆 × 𝐴 × 𝑆 →
[0, 1], which specifies the probability of transitioning from one
state to another after taking a specific action. For instance,
in traffic routing, 𝑃(𝑠, 𝑎, 𝑠′ ) could represent the likelihood of
reaching a less congested road segment 𝑠′ after taking action
𝑎 in state 𝑠.
∙ R: A reward function, 𝑅 ∶ 𝑆 × 𝐴 × 𝑆 → ℝ, which provides
feedback to the agent for transitioning between states. In this
study, rewards are shaped based on traffic conditions, such as
reduced waiting times on road segments.
∙ 𝛾: The discount factor, 0 ≤ 𝛾 < 1, which determines the
importance of future rewards relative to immediate rewards.
A higher 𝛾 encourages the agent to prioritize long-term gains
over short-term benefits.
The objective of an MDP is to identify a policy 𝜋 ∶ 𝑆 → 𝐴 that
maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward, given by:

Deep Q-Network (DQN)

Building on the advantages of DQL and DQN, we implement the
DQL-PER algorithm, which incorporates the following enhancements. By integrating DQL’s two-network approach to mitigate
overestimation bias with the replay architecture originally introduced by DQN, DQL-PER leverages both stability and sampling
efficiency. DQL ensures more accurate Q-value estimation, while
the prioritized replay concept accelerates convergence by focusing on high-Temporal Difference (TD)-error transitions. This
synergy not only reduces bias and enhances learning efficiency,
but also equips DQL-PER with the scalability needed to handle
large-scale urban traffic networks more effectively.

experiences uniformly from the replay buffer, PER assigns a

when it reaches convergence, which is defined as the moment at
which it starts to provide consistent and steady performance. We
provide a convergence threshold to quantitatively determine this
moment. This cutoff point is set at 90% of the algorithm’s mean
of the final awards. The algorithm is said to have converged when
its performance rises above this point. The earliest episode where
the accumulated rewards either match or surpass this threshold
is then identified as the convergence episode. This methodology
offers a simple criterion for evaluating the algorithm’s convergence and provides an effective assessment of its learning curve.

Conclusion

In this research, double Q-learning with prioritized experience
replay (DQL-PER) was investigated as a solution to the enduring
problem of optimizing vehicle routing in urban traffic networks.
The SUMO platform provided a dynamic and accurate testing
environment, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of the algorithm. The results demonstrate that DQL-PER performs more
accurately and efficiently than Q-learning, double Q-learning
(DQL), and deep Q-network (DQN), particularly in handling
high-dimensional state spaces– a critical feature for intricate
urban traffic systems. Its reduced overestimation bias and accelerated convergence highlight its potential for real-time traffic
management applications.
The scalability study further underscored DQL-PER’s capacity to
handle increasingly complex network structures, showcasing its
applicability in large-scale urban environments. This is particularly important given the growing size and complexity of modern
cities. Additionally, the algorithm’s improved learning efficiency
and convergence speed make it well-suited for optimizing traffic
flows in various urban contexts.
However, the study also identified certain limitations of the DQLPER algorithm. First, while the scalability of DQL-PER was
evident, the computational overhead increased significantly for
larger networks due to the prioritization mechanism and the need
to maintain two Q-value functions. This poses a challenge for realtime applications, where frequent updates are essential. Second,
the algorithm’s reliance on accurate reward shaping and hyperparameter tuning introduces sensitivity to these factors, potentially
affecting its robustness in diverse scenarios. Third, achieving
stable convergence required extensive training episodes, limiting
its adaptability in dynamic traffic environments where immediate
responsiveness is crucial. These limitations present avenues for
improvement in future work.
To enhance its real-world applicability, future research could
focus on integrating DQL-PER with incident management systems and adaptive signal control strategies, enabling a more
holistic approach to urban traffic optimization. Furthermore,
testing the algorithm with real-world traffic data and simulating
scenarios with varying patterns and disruptions would help validate its effectiveness under practical conditions. By addressing
the identified limitations, DQL-PER has the potential to further
enhance its performance and scalability, making it a promising
tool for real-world urban traffic management.
